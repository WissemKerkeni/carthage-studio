# World — CARTHAGE SAGA

**Status:** `PROPOSED`. Historical basis in `story/historical-dossier.md`.
Evidence tiers `[A]` documented · `[B]` likely · `[C]` disputed · `[F]` invented.

## Setting

`[A]` The western Mediterranean, c. 825 BC – 146 BC. Scale: a sea and its coasts — the Levant,
North Africa, Sicily, Sardinia, Iberia, Italy, Gaul, and the Atlantic edge. The series never
leaves the world of ships and their one-day hops between anchorages, except when Hannibal
deliberately leaves it and walks.

## Physical world

| Place | `Tier` | What it is on screen |
|---|---|---|
| **Carthage / Qart-ḥadašt** | `[A]` | A triangular peninsula closed by a narrow neck, walled across it. Hill of **Byrsa** with the temple of **Eshmun** at the top (sixty steps `[B]`). Dense multi-storey housing on the slopes — up to six floors `[A]`, laundry, shouting, no privacy. The **Megara** suburb: walled garden estates, orchards, irrigation. The **tophet** in a quiet walled precinct near the sea. The **cothon**: rectangular merchant harbour opening into a circular naval basin, an island command post at its centre, ship-sheds for 220 hulls `[A]`. Behind: the **Lake of Tunis**, shallow, stinking, full of fish and flamingos. |
| **The chora** | `[A]` | Cape Bon and the Bagradas valley. Irrigated estate agriculture — vines, olives, figs, pomegranates, almonds — the most scientific farming in the western world `[A]`, worked by Libyan tenants and slaves. This is where the money actually comes from, and the series says so repeatedly. |
| **Utica** | `[A]` | Older than Carthage, three hours up the coast, permanently resentful. In Book One it is the snob; in Book Four it massacres its Carthaginian garrison and offers itself to Rome; in Book Eight it defects to Rome first. |
| **Tyre** | `[A]` | An island fortress with two harbours, purple-dye stink downwind, and Assyrian tribute-collectors in the counting houses. Book One's opening. Revisited once, in ch. 180, six hundred and thirty years later. |
| **Sicily** | `[A]` | The wrestling-ground. Greek cities on the east and south, Punic ports — **Motya**, **Panormus**, **Lilybaeum**, **Drepana** — in the west; **Mount Eryx** with its temple of Astarte, above the clouds. Volcanic, fertile, and permanently at war. |
| **Sardinia** | `[A]` | Grain, silver, and nuraghi. Carthage's estate; in 237 BC, Rome's theft. |
| **Iberia** | `[A]` | Silver. The mines at **Cástulo** and the Rio Tinto; **Gadir**, the oldest Phoenician city in the west; **New Carthage**, Hasdrubal's planned capital, with the best harbour in the western sea and a shallow lagoon that a Roman will one day wade across. Inland: the Celtiberian and Iberian peoples, falcata swords, hill forts, mercenary contracts. |
| **The Alps** | `[A]` | Fifteen days of it. Gorges where the ambush comes from above; a col with a view over the Po plain; nine days of descent that kills more than the climb. |
| **Italy** | `[A]` | The Po plain (Gallic, hostile to Rome); the Apennine spine; **Lake Trasimene**'s fog; the Apulian plain and the **Aufidus** at Cannae with its dry summer wind, the Volturnus, full of dust; the toe of **Bruttium** where the last fifteen years grind out. |
| **Numidia** | `[A]` | Open high plains south and west. Horses without saddle or bridle `[A]`, transhumant herding, two great confederations — **Massylii** (east, Gaia then Masinissa) and **Masaesyli** (west, Syphax). |

## Society — Carthage

`[A]` **Citizenship** is a Punic-descent, property-holding, city-dwelling status. Below it: resident
foreigners, Libyphoenicians (mixed Punic-Libyan populations of the coastal towns), Libyan subjects,
and slaves — war captives, debt slaves, and the purchased.

`[A]` **Government** — see the dossier, Part Four. On screen the series treats it as four rooms:

- the **Senate house** (Adirim), where policy is made by men who are simultaneously the state's
  largest creditors;
- the **court of the Hundred and Four**, life-tenured, whose entire purpose is to prosecute
  generals — a room the audience learns to dread;
- the **Assembly** in the open square, loud, bought, and occasionally decisive;
- the **counting house**, which is the real one.

`[A]` Aristotle's complaint is the series' running joke and running horror: **offices are bought.**
A man is elected suffete because he can afford to be, and recoups it from the office.

`[B]` **The money.** Carthage is late to coinage (from the late 5th c. BC, initially to pay Sicilian
mercenaries `[A]`) and never fully monetises its internal economy. Wealth is ships, land, warehouses,
contracts, and the tribute of the chora. Silver is a plot object in every Book.

## Society — the subject peoples

`[A]`/`[B]` The Libyans of the chora pay tribute — in wartime, reportedly half the harvest `[C]` —
and supply the infantry that wins Carthage's battles. They are not citizens and cannot become
citizens. They revolt in 396, with Agathocles in 310, in 241, and finally with Rome. The series
treats every Carthaginian golden age as a Libyan extraction, and says so through the **tent of
Iyarut** `[F]` rather than through narration.

## Technology level

`[A]` Iron age, bronze fittings, Phoenician shipbuilding at the top of the world's art:
mortise-and-tenon hulls, the quinquereme, and — attested by the **Marsala wreck** — **numbered
prefabricated parts**, so a fleet can be replaced in months. That single fact is a major plot
driver in Book Three: both sides can rebuild navies faster than they can train sailors, so the war
is decided by drowned men, not lost ships.

Other: the catapult, invented at Syracuse in 399 BC and used against Motya `[A]`. Siege towers,
mining, the ram. Purple dye, glass, ivory, terracotta moulds. Deep-plough estate agriculture,
irrigation, grafting and manuring — Mago's twenty-eight books `[A]`. Cothon dry docks.
**No stirrups.** **No horseshoes.** Numidians ride with neither saddle nor bridle `[A]`.

## The supernatural / power system

**There is none.** This is a hard rule (`story/story-bible.md` §9.4).

- **What it is:** religion, and nothing else. Baal Hammon, Tanit, Melqart, Eshmun, Astarte; later
  Demeter and Kore, adopted in 396 BC after a plague `[A]`.
- **Who uses it:** priests, who are also political officeholders; and every frightened person.
- **How acquired:** by birth, purchase and vow.
- **What it costs:** vows are contractual. *molk* — the giving of the thing promised — is a debt
  to a god, and the series treats the tophet as the place where the worst kind of debt is settled `[C]`.
- **Hard limits:** the camera never confirms a god. Omens are shown as objects — a liver, a flight
  of birds, a fire — and the *interpretation* is always a human political act.
- **Who polices it:** the priesthoods, and the families who own them.

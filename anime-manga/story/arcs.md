# Arc & Chapter Breakdown — CARTHAGE SAGA

**Status:** `PROPOSED`. **4 Seasons · 8 Books · 200 chapters · c. 825–146 BC.**
Narrative text: `story/THE-STORY.md`. Facts: `story/historical-dossier.md`.
`[A]` documented · `[B]` likely · `[C]` disputed · `[F]` invented.

**★** = must exist in any cut. **◆** = aftermath chapter; may never be cut (bible §9.5).

> **Renumbering notice, `RETCON`.** An earlier draft ran to 212 chapters ending in 183 BC.
> The series now ends with the destruction of Carthage in 146 BC, and Books One–Two are compressed
> so the pre-Punic history is 30% of the runtime rather than 45%. **Chapters 1–22 are unchanged**,
> so all existing chapter-1 production work remains valid. Concordance at the foot of this file.

**100 episodes.** The chapters-per-episode figure falls every season: the series physically slows
down as it approaches its protagonist and again as it approaches the end. Chapter page budgets rise
to match — Books One–Two average 24 pages, Books Three–Six 32, the Cannae and Six Days sequences 40+.

**Season 1 has no Hannibal in it** and that is the one structural risk in the whole plan. It is
mitigated three ways: a 90-second **anime-only cold open** on episode 1 (see below); five
self-contained protagonists so no viewer is waiting for someone; and a season finale that hands the
audience the war the whole series is about.

> **Manga/anime divergence, ch. 1.** The manga opens exactly as built: Tyre, the rope walk, Sosylos'
> voice. **Anime E01 adds a cold open** — 90 seconds, no context: a nine-year-old's hand held down on
> a sacrificial victim, a man's voice asking him to swear, the boy swearing. Cut to black. Cut to
> Tyre, six hundred years earlier. The scene is replayed in full and in context in **ch. 116**, at
> the end of Season 2. Do not extend the cold open; do not explain it; no character is named.

# SEASON ONE
*c. 825–264 BC · chapters 1–60 · 26 episodes*

**Function:** establish the debt that runs for six hundred years, and build — piece by piece, each
piece a reaction to a disaster the audience has just watched — the machine that will one day produce
Hannibal and then destroy him.

# SEASON TWO
*264–237 BC · chapters 61–117 · 25 episodes*

**Function:** make the audience understand Rome — not as a monster, but as the one state in the
world that does not recognise defeat as a category. Then present the bill.

## BOOK THREE — THE SEA BETWEEN (ch. 61–92) — the First Punic War

| Ch. | Title | Content | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| 61–65 ★ | **Both Letters** | Two councils, filmed in parallel, each reasoning correctly from its own premises into a war neither planned. Rome's Assembly votes for it expecting profit. Ch. 64 ◆ ★: the Carthaginian commander who avoided a battle and saved his garrison is **crucified for it** — the court from ch. 35, killing someone the audience likes. | `[A]`/`[C]` |
| 66–69 ★ | **Agrigentum** | Seven months; plague in both camps; the garrison escapes competently and leaves the civilians. **Twenty-five thousand sold.** Built around one family on a beach and a contractor sorting them by price. *This is how the Greek west learns what Rome is, and no battle report mentions it.* | `[A]` |
| 70–76 ★ | **The Raven, and the Sea** | Rome copies a wrecked Punic hull — built from numbered prefabricated parts, legible as a set of instructions — and builds a fleet. **Milkat** does not laugh at it. **Mylae** and the corvus: the most technical form of warfare in the world turned into an infantry fight on a plank. Then **Ecnomus** (73–76), ~680 ships, filmed almost entirely from below deck where nobody can see anything. | `[A]`/`[C]` |
| 77–83 ★ | **Regulus, and the Storm** | Three chapters establishing a Roman the audience would trust with their money. Adys; Tunis; and ch. 80 ★: **terms so harsh no state could accept them, offered kindly** — he cannot imagine why a beaten people would refuse. Then **Xanthippus** (81–82), who rebuilds a broken army in a month, destroys Regulus, collects his fee and leaves mid-chapter because he is a professional. Ch. 83 ◆ ★: **the storm** — 384 ships of 464, ~100,000 men, no enemy. Eleven pages without dialogue. | `[A]`/`[C]` |
| 84–86 ★ | **Drepana** | Panormus: Metellus breaks the elephant corps with a ditch and patience. **Adherbal's** victory at Drepana; the sacred chickens; Phintias. Carthage sweeps Rome from the sea — **and then cuts the naval budget, because the war looks won.** A reasonable decision on the evidence available. | `[A]`/`[C]` |
| 87–92 ★ | **Eryx** | 247 BC: **Hamilcar Barca** takes command; in the same dispatch bag, news that his first son is born. Six years of raiding from Heircte and Eryx with an army nobody pays; never defeated, entirely irrelevant, and he knows it. Ch. 91: **the Aegates** — Milkat's last chapter, and the series does not say whether he is picked up. Ch. 92 ★★: **Sign It.** Hamilcar, undefeated, is ordered by his own government to negotiate his surrender — and refuses to disarm his men personally, handing that duty to **Gisco**, which causes everything in Book Four. | `[A]` |

## BOOK FOUR — TRUCELESS (ch. 93–117)

| Ch. | Title | Content | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| 93–99 ★ | **The Wages** | Twenty thousand unpaid men, fifteen languages, an empty treasury and a negotiation that teaches the army the number is not fixed. **A procurement failure before it is a crime.** Ch. 96 ★: **Autaritus**, the Gaul who speaks Punic and therefore controls what forty thousand men believe is being offered. Ch. 97–98 ★: **Spendius**, who will be crucified if there is a settlement and therefore requires that there not be one. Ch. 99 ★★: **Mathos** makes the case against Carthage, once, quietly, in good Punic, to an honest man who has no answer. | `[A]`/`[B]` |
| 100–105 ★ | **Africa Rises** | Seventy thousand Libyans; village women giving up jewellery to fund it. Utica and Hippacritae massacre their garrisons; **Utica offers itself to Rome, and Rome honourably refuses** (102 ★ — the audience should be unsettled by how much they like Rome here). Hanno fails at Utica. Ch. 104 ★: **the Bagradas** — the first Barcid battle plan in the series, used on Carthage's own soldiers. Ch. 105 ★: **Naravas** rides into the enemy camp alone to change sides and is promised a daughter; **Iyarut** rides behind him while his cousin **Massiv** rides with Mathos. | `[A]`/`[F]` |
| 106–112 ★★ | **Seven Hundred** | Hamilcar's mercy-as-a-weapon works, so Spendius and Autaritus destroy the possibility of mercy in one afternoon, and put it to a vote so that every man present is complicit. Hamilcar answers with elephants. Ch. 109 ◆: nine pages, no dialogue. **The Saw** (110–111): forty thousand men eat their horses, then their slaves, then surrender. Ch. 112 ★: **Mathos torn apart in the street** — filmed from the crowd's point of view, staying with a woman who has lost two sons and is entirely justified. | `[A]` |
| 113–114 ★★ | **Sardinia** | Rome declares Carthage's attempt to retake its own province an act of war and takes Sardinia, Corsica and 1,200 talents more. Polybius' verdict quoted in narration. Hamilcar receives the news, asks the man to repeat the figure, says thank you, and goes on with what he was doing. **His six-year-old son is in the room.** | `[A]` |
| 115 | **A Boy of Nine** | Hannibal introduced — not as a prodigy, as a child trying to get his father to look at him. | `[F]` on `[A]` |
| 116 ★★★ | **The Altar** | **The oath.** Shot plainly, no music. Then the cut that is the point of the chapter: four officers waiting outside. *"Did he do it?" — "Of course he did it."* They go back to work; the policy was decided a year ago in another city by men who have never met this child. | `[A]`/`[C]` |
| 117 ◆ | **The Long Coast** | The army marches to the Straits because Carthage cannot spare ships. Humiliation measured in footsteps. **Bostar** counts everything. **End of Season Two.** | `[B]` |

# SEASON THREE
*237–216 BC · chapters 118–157 · 24 episodes*

**Function:** Hannibal. Built, then unleashed. Ends at the high-water mark of his life, which is
also the moment the audience realises he cannot win.

## BOOK FIVE — SILVER (ch. 118–136)

| Ch. | Title | Content | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| 118–128 | **Silver** | Nine years building a private state: mines, tribal treaties, hostages, Akra Leuke, bullion shipped home to buy the Assembly. Ch. 122 ◆: **hostages** — the chiefs' children are Hannibal's companions and his family's collateral, and he understands that by fourteen. Ch. 124 ★: **Sosylos** arrives to teach Greek; the narrator becomes a character, 123 chapters in. Ch. 126: a small command handled well and dully — competence before genius. Ch. 127–128 ★: **the river.** Hamilcar dies covering his sons' retreat; both traditions in one frame, unexplained. | `[A]`/`[C]` |
| 129–136 ★ | **Hasdrubal, the Treaty, and Saguntum** | **Hasdrubal the Fair**: rule by marriage, money and patience; New Carthage founded; the Iberians acclaim a foreign occupier as their own commander. Ch. 131 ★: **the Ebro treaty** — deliberately vague, signed by two parties who each believe the vagueness favours them; two chapters later a Roman and a Carthaginian give flatly contradictory accounts and the series never adjudicates. Ch. 132 ★: **the road not taken** — Hasdrubal tells Hannibal what Hamilcar actually intended from the day he landed. Ch. 133 ★: a knife in a corridor; the killer dies under torture, smiling. Ch. 134 ★★: **acclamation** — the army shouts, Carthage confirms, and **Hanno the Great argues against it and is right about everything except the outcome.** Ch. 135: **the Tagus**, the first recognisably Hannibalic victory, planned out loud the evening before so the audience can watch it arrive. Ch. 136 ★★: **Saguntum**, eight months in one chapter of attrition; the plunder shipped home to buy the Senate; Fabius' toga; *take which you please*; **war.** | `[A]`/`[C]` |

## BOOK SIX — THE ROAD (ch. 137–157)

| Ch. | Title | Content | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| 137 ★ | **Leaving New Carthage** | Iberians to Africa, Africans to Iberia: every soldier a thousand miles from his village and every village within reach of someone's garrison. *"They did not volunteer. Their fathers signed."* | `[A]` |
| 138 ◆ | **Imilce** | The wife the sources barely name. One chapter, no melodrama, never mentioned again — because he never mentioned her again either. | `[C]`/`[F]` |
| 139–140 ★ | **The Rhône** | A night crossing upstream and a fire behind the Gauls; **elephants on earthed rafts**, two of which walk out along the bottom; Scipio arriving three days late at an empty camp. | `[A]` |
| 141–145 ★★ | **The Alps** | Five chapters in which **nothing heroic happens**. Ambush from above in the gorges; guides who lie; a tribe that gives hostages and attacks the baggage three days later; the rockfall broken with fire (and two soldiers watching a man pour sour wine on a hot rock with complete contempt); the col, and Italy; then nine days of descent that kill more than the climb. **Safan** loses his mule, his brother and his belief that anyone is in control. | `[A]`/`[C]` |
| 146 | **Ticinus** | A cavalry skirmish, a consul down, and a seventeen-year-old riding into it — with a Ligurian slave standing silently in frame `[C]`. Scipio introduced as a frightened boy doing something stupid and brave. | `[A]`/`[B]` |
| 147–148 ★ | **Trebia** | Cold water before breakfast; Mago out of the reed beds; and ten thousand Romans punching clean through and marching away in good order, **because they are extremely good soldiers**. Then the winter that kills nearly every elephant. | `[A]` |
| 149 ★ | **The Marsh** | Four days and three nights in flood water, sleeping on dead pack animals. **The eye** — no heroics, an infection and a surgeon with no good options. | `[A]` |
| 150–151 ★★ | **Trasimene** | Fog on a lake road; three hours of killing in a space with no room to form a line. **Caeso Rutilius** survives by lying under a corpse, breathing through a scabbard. | `[A]` |
| 152 ★★ | **The Delayer** | Fabius: burn Roman farms, shadow the army, refuse every battle. The cattle and torches. And the chapter's real subject — **he is despised by Rome for the strategy that will save it, and knows it, and continues.** The series' portrait of political courage, and it is given to a Roman. | `[A]` |
| 153–156 ★★★ | **Cannae** | One day, four chapters, eight hours. **153** the ground, the wind, the water, the two consuls disagreeing like professionals. **154** the crescent giving way on purpose, filmed from the second rank of a Gallic company. **155** the cavalry's long, dusty, boring ride around the back of the world. **156** three hours of killing at arm's length in a space too tight to raise a sword, from four viewpoints, one of them Roman — and Hannibal on foot behind his own line, doing almost nothing, because he did all of it before dawn. | `[A]` |
| 157 ◆ ★★★ | **Use It** | Maharbal's line; Hannibal's answer, which is about siege trains, supply and politics, and is *correct*, and loses the war anyway. Then Rome: no ransom, no negotiation, a law against public mourning, two legions raised from slaves — and the Senate going out of the city to **thank the consul who lost sixty-seven thousand men for not despairing of the republic.** **End of Season Three.** | `[A]`/`[C]` |

# SEASON FOUR
*216–146 BC · chapters 158–200 · 25 episodes*

**Function:** the long defeat, and then the seventy years of consequence. Hannibal dies in ch. 182
with eighteen chapters still to run, and the series' argument is that **he is in every one of them.**

## BOOK SEVEN — THE LONG DEFEAT (ch. 158–184)

| Ch. | Title | Content | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| 158–162 | **Sixteen Years** | Capua defects and becomes base and hostage; Syracuse, Archimedes, and a soldier who kills him over a diagram; *Hannibal ad portas* and the field auctioned in the Forum at full price `[C]`; Capua falls and its ruling class is executed, which tells every Italian city what defection costs. Ch. 161: the Scipio brothers killed at the Upper Baetis after their Celtiberians are bought away. Ch. 162 ★★: **Hanno's full-page argument** against reinforcing Italy — *he is fighting his father's war; the day he wins it he comes home with sixty thousand men who love him, and we are a monarchy by autumn* — which the audience cannot dismiss, having watched Bomilcar in ch. 58. | `[A]`/`[C]` |
| 163–167 ★ | **The Other One** | Scipio given Spain at 25 because nobody else will take it. **New Carthage in a day** through the lagoon — the tide table from a fisherman in the morning, the dream of Neptune in the afternoon, in that order. **The hostages sent home free**: Hannibal's own method, used better, by a man who understands that it is politics. Baecula; **Ilipa**; the mutiny at Sucro crushed. Marcellus killed in a scouting ambush at sixty, and his ashes sent back. | `[A]` |
| 168–169 ★★ | **His Brother's Head** | The intercepted dispatch; **Claudius Nero's** secret march, 400 km in seven days, the towns putting food into the soldiers' hands as they walk; Hasdrubal reading two sets of trumpet calls and understanding exactly what has happened; the Metaurus; and a sack thrown over a palisade at dawn. He opens it himself, puts the cloth back, and does not speak again that day. | `[A]` |
| 170–172 ★ | **Recalled** | Mago dies at sea. **Masinissa** goes over — the prince raised in Carthage, whose betrothed was given to his rival. Scipio in Africa: the burning of the sleeping camps during peace talks, filmed at full length, with Scipio's completely honest answer afterwards about the difference between a truce and talks. Sophonisba and the poison, cut to nine words. Ch. 172 ★★: **the bronze tablets at Croton** — Hannibal has his whole campaign carved in Punic and Greek before he sails, *because otherwise no one will ever know what actually happened*, and narration states that this is why the series can exist. | `[A]`/`[C]` |
| 173–176 ★★★ | **Zama** | The parley: two professionals who understand each other perfectly, and Scipio's closing question — *if you had beaten me, would you have taken those terms?* On the flank, **Safan** and **Iyarut** look at each other across a hundred paces of dust, six hundred years of entangled family between them, and nothing comes of it. Then: eighty untrained elephants and every trumpet in the Roman army; three lines used properly; the hardest infantry fighting of the war, decided by cavalry coming back from a pursuit. | `[A]`/`[F]` |
| 177–179 ★★ | **The Audit** | The terms, and the clause that matters: **no war in Africa without Rome's leave.** Then 196 BC — Hannibal as suffete, going through the books, exposing the embezzlement, making the Hundred and Four elective and annual, and **paying Rome in full without a new tax, out of money that had been stolen.** He wins the argument he was born into, and the men he ruins write to Rome. | `[A]` |
| 180–181 ★ | **A Man Without a City** | He does not let Carthage choose whether to surrender him. **Tyre after six hundred years.** Antiochus, who wants his name and not his advice; the review of the golden army and the one joke anyone recorded him making; a fleet at last, and Side; Magnesia; Crete; Bithynia; the snake-pots, and the only time in the season that he laughs. | `[A]`/`[C]` |
| 182 ★★ | **Libyssa** | Soldiers in the garden. He asks how many and at which exits, works it out in two seconds, and asks for the cup. `[C]` The alternative versions and all three dates are reported by a servant in the next chapter, unreconciled. | `[C]` |
| 183 ◆ | **Liternum** | The same season: Scipio dies in retirement, prosecuted by his own city, having torn his account-books in half in the Senate rather than justify himself. Two men who saved their states, dead in exile from them within months of each other. | `[A]`/`[C]` |
| 184 ★★ | **Seven Books** | Sosylos finishes. The cord on the table; nineteen knots. *Nobody will ever read this. I wrote it anyway. That is also a kind of ledger.* | `[A]`/`[F]` |

## BOOK EIGHT — THE ASHES (ch. 185–200)

> **The narration changes hands.** Sosylos' seven books are lost — the series shows them being sold
> for the blank backs of the sheets. **Polybius of Megalopolis** takes over: an Achaean hostage in
> Italy, tutor and friend to the man who will burn Carthage, whose forty books survive *because he
> was in the right house.* He says so in his first lines. That handover is the series' closing
> argument, and it is why Book Eight exists.

| Ch. | Title | Content | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| 185 ★ | **The Debt** | Fifty years of instalments paid out of the revenue system Hannibal built in the one year he was allowed to govern, by the men who drove him out. **191 BC: Carthage offers to pay the whole remainder at once, and Rome refuses** — *a debt that is paid is a leash that is dropped.* Polybius reads the Croton tablets in the temple at Croton. | `[A]` |
| 186 ★ | **The Boy Who Was Raised Here** | **Masinissa**, 54 years a king. The method: annex, wait for the appeal, let the Roman commission find for you or go home undecided. Seventy times. And one long scene in which the last man alive who met Hannibal is asked what he was like — *short; very polite; he remembered everybody's name, and I thought it was kindness until I realised it was inventory.* | `[A]`/`[F]` |
| 187 ★ | **Figs** | Cato at eighty, and the fig on the Senate floor — staged as told, then undercut on screen by two senators, one of whom says it was bought in the forum that morning and the other of whom says it does not matter. Against him, **Scipio Nasica**: preserve Carthage *because* it is dangerous, since a Rome with nothing to fear will turn on itself. He is right, and he loses the vote. | `[A]`/`[C]` |

## Structural rules (unchanged)

1. **Every Book ends with an institution created or destroyed**, not with a battle: One the
   suffeteship; Two the mercenary system; Three the surrender order; Four the oath; Five the
   acclamation of a family's heir; Six Rome's refusal to negotiate; Seven the abolition of the
   Hundred and Four's life tenure; Eight the province of Africa.
2. **Five "founding story retold" scenes** — ch. 21, 41, 52, 87, 172 — each told differently by
   someone who needs it to mean something different. The audience, who saw chapters 1–18, watches
   the truth erode in real time, and then in ch. 200 watches the real thing happen again.
3. **The aftermath rule is absolute.** ◆ chapters cannot be cut in adaptation.
4. **Rome's first appearance is ch. 32** and Rome is not a threat until ch. 60. The audience should
   experience Rome the way Carthage did — as a minor agricultural power that could not possibly matter.
5. **Two narrators, one handover.** Sosylos is voice-only until ch. 124 and has one wordless
   appearance in ch. 1. Polybius takes the narration in ch. 185 and appears on screen from ch. 196.

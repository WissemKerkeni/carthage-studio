# CARTHAGE SAGA
### カルタゴ・サガ — the narrative text

**Status:** `PROPOSED`. This is the story. Scripts derive from here; canon derives from
`story/story-bible.md`; facts derive from `story/historical-dossier.md`.

**Structure.** 4 seasons · 8 Books · 200 chapters · c. 825–146 BC. Each season is exactly two
Books. Two narrators: **Sosylos of Sparta** (Books One–Seven), whose seven books were lost, and
**Polybius of Megalopolis** (Book Eight), whose forty survived. The handover between them is the
series' closing argument.

**How to read this file.** Continuous narrative, Book by Book, following the chapter map in
`story/arcs.md`. Key scenes are written out in full. Connective passages narrate at speed.
Where the story makes a choice about disputed history, a bracketed note says so — those notes
are for the production team and never appear on the page.

---

> *I am Sosylos, of Sparta, and I taught a boy Greek.*
>
> *He is dead now, and so is his city, and so is nearly everyone in what follows. I have written
> seven books about him. I expect that none of them will survive, because the people who will
> decide what survives are the people he spent his life fighting, and they write very well.*
>
> *So let me begin before him. Let me begin where he would have begun, because he was a man who
> never explained a battle without first explaining the ground.*
>
> *I will not see the end of this. Someone else finishes it. He is a Greek too, and he is
> better placed than I am, and that is the whole difference between us.*
>
> *The ground is this: a city that was founded by people running for their lives, on land it did
> not own, with money it had stolen from a corpse.*

# BOOK ONE — THE NEW CITY
*c. 825–800 BC · chapters 1–22 · **Season 1**, first half*

## 1. The rope walk

Tyre smelled of dead shellfish, and everybody who lived there had stopped noticing.

The murex vats ran along the windward shore so the stink would blow out to sea, and it never did;
it hung over the island like a second roof. Under it, in the long shadow of the temple, the rope
walk ran four hundred paces from the harbour wall to the timber yard, and in it the family of
Yatonbaal laid cord as it had laid cord for six generations, walking backwards, hemp turning in
their fingers, laying yarn into strand and strand into rope.

Elishat was fourteen and could splice better than her father. This was not a matter of pride; it
was a matter of small hands. She was working when the priest came.

He came without a retinue, which was the first strange thing. Zakarbaal was the second man in the
city — priest of Melqart, husband of the king's sister — and he came alone, down the rope walk,
lifting the hem of a very good robe over the tar, and he stopped and watched them work for the time
it takes to lay ten paces of cable.

Then he said: "How much rope can you make in a month, and can you do it without anyone knowing?"

Elishat's father did not look up. In Tyre, that was the polite answer to a question like that.

"That depends on what you tell the harbour master."

"Nothing," said the priest. "I intend to tell him nothing at all."

> *[Production note: the first chapter of the series contains no violence, no exposition of politics,
> and no named historical event. It establishes three things: the smell of the city, the trade of the
> family the audience will follow for six hundred years, and the fact that something is being
> prepared in secret. Chapter 1 ends on the rope.]*

## 2–3. The second man

What was being prepared was an escape, and Zakarbaal was preparing it badly, because he was a
careful man who had never been frightened before and did not know how it was done.

The facts, as the city understood them, were these. The old king had died and left a son and a
daughter. The son, Pygmalion, was young. The daughter, Elissa, had married her uncle the high
priest, which put the temple treasury and the throne in the same family and in two different hands.
Tyre paid tribute to Assyria now — had paid it for a generation — and the tribute came out of the
temple as often as out of the palace, and the accounts of who had paid what, and who therefore owed
whom, had become the only politics anybody in Tyre practised.

`[C] The sources say Pygmalion killed Acerbas/Zakarbaal for his hidden gold. The series keeps the
murder and makes the motive fiscal rather than simply greedy: the temple was refusing to fund the
tribute, and the king could not be king without it.`

Elissa was told in a small room by a chamberlain who had been given money to break it gently, and
who broke it gently, and who she understood — while he was still speaking — had been given the
money by her brother.

She did not weep in front of him. Later, in the story Carthage told about her, she wept
magnificently. In the series she says four words, and they are about the ships.

## 4. Sacks

They went out on a night with no moon, eight ships, and a hundred and forty people who had all
decided separately that Pygmalion would come for them next.

Off Cyprus, with the pursuit lights on the horizon, Elissa had the crews bring up the sacks. They
were heavy, corded, sealed; everyone aboard had watched them loaded and everyone aboard believed
they held the temple silver. She stood at the rail and spoke a prayer for her husband and had them
thrown into the sea, one after another, forty of them, and the men watched forty fortunes go under
the water.

Then she told them what was in the sacks.

Sand. The silver was in the bilge, under the ballast, where it had been since the second night.

And the men understood, standing on a deck in the dark, that they had just been seen by the king's
pursuit to throw the royal treasure into the sea — that they were now, every one of them, men who
had robbed the crown of Tyre and destroyed the evidence, and that there was no longer any such
place as home.

One of the sailors began to laugh. It was not a good laugh. Elishat, fourteen, holding a stay,
watched a hundred and forty people become a single thing, and understood, much later, that this was
what founding a city was.

> *[This is the series' first statement of method. Elissa does not inspire loyalty; she manufactures
> it by removing the alternative. Hannibal will do a version of this in chapter 137, exchanging
> Iberian and African troops across the sea so that every soldier's family is a hostage. The
> rhyme is the point.]*

## 5–6. Eighty

At Cyprus a priest of Astarte came aboard and named his price: the priesthood of his god in the new
city, for his line, for ever. Elissa agreed in less than a minute. It was the first office sold in
Carthaginian history, and Carthage did not exist yet.

They also took eighty women off the shore.

The sources are brisk about this. They were, we are told, temple women, or women of the harbour;
they were taken so that the men would have wives; the city needed children.

The series films it from the shore. It films the boats coming in, and the running, and a woman
called — in this story — Bititt, who is nineteen and is carrying a basket of someone else's
washing, and who is on a ship by evening. She gets fourteen chapters. She marries a rope-maker's
cousin, buries two children, teaches her surviving son Cypriot Greek, and dies in chapter 20 of an
infected tooth, having never once said she forgave anybody.

`[F] Bititt is invented. The eighty women are in the sources. What is invented is that anyone
bothered to ask what it was like.`

## 7–9. Rent

Africa, when they came to it, was not empty. This surprised nobody except later storytellers.

There was a bay with a lagoon behind it and a hill above it and a long shallow shelf where ships
could be drawn up, and it belonged to people. Up the coast, three hours under oars, there was
already a Phoenician city — Utica — old, established, and entirely uninterested in helping.

So Elissa did what her husband's temple had done for four hundred years. She bought.

The negotiation took eleven days. It was conducted through two interpreters and a Libyan trader who
was cheating everyone, and it was about grazing rights, water, the lagoon fish, the right to cut
timber, the obligation to give shelter in war, and — above all — **the annual payment**.

Not a purchase. A tenancy.

`[A] Carthage paid rent to the Libyans for its land, reportedly until the mid-sixth century BC.
This is the single most important fact in Book One and the series builds the whole of Book Two
on the moment it stops being paid.`

Tikat was there. She was the daughter of one of the chiefs, twenty years old, and she was there
because her father wanted someone who could count to be watching the foreigners count. What she
remembered afterwards, and told her grandchildren, was not the bargaining. It was that at the end
of it the foreign queen asked — through two interpreters, badly — what the hill was called.

Nobody had ever asked. It was the hill. It was called the hill.

The foreigners named it Bosra, which meant citadel, and in six hundred years a Greek would hear that
word, think it was his own word for an ox-hide, and invent a story so good that it would outlive
every person in this chapter.

## 10–14. The first winter, and the old city's table

The first winter nearly finished them. Three died of a bad well. There was a fight, with knives,
about whether to sail back to Tyre and throw themselves on Pygmalion's mercy, and the fight was
settled by the simple observation that they had all been seen throwing the treasury into the sea.

In the spring, Elishat's father tied a knot in a length of ship's cable and hung it on the wall.

"What's that for?"

"So we know we did it."

`[F] The knotted cord. One knot per generation. It is the only object in the series that is present
in both the first chapter and the last, and the audience will be told in chapter 184 that it burned.`

They dug a harbour, badly. They planted, worse; Tikat had to show them that the barley they had
brought from the Levant would not do here and that the land wanted something else. They learned
from the people whose rent they were paying, and within sixty years they would be teaching the
world how to farm, and within two hundred they would own the teachers.

Utica had them to dinner once. It was a careful, generous, insulting evening. The Uticans asked
about the temple at Tyre, and about the king, and about whether the new settlement had a name yet.

"Qart-ḥadašt," said Elissa. *The new city.*

Their host let a pause go by. "New," he said pleasantly, "compared to what?"

## 15–18. The price of land

The trouble came, as trouble does, in the form of a reasonable proposal.

A Libyan chief — the sources call him Iarbas, and the series uses the name — offered marriage. It
was a good offer. It would have ended the rent. It would have made the settlement a client of the
interior rather than a tenant, and the council, meeting in the half-built precinct on the hill,
was overwhelmingly in favour.

Elissa was not, and the series is careful about why. It is not chastity, and it is not grief, though
both are available to her and she uses both in public. It is this: a marriage makes the city a
possession that can be inherited. A city that belongs to a husband can be given to a son, and traded,
and split. She had watched exactly that happen in Tyre to a kingdom far older and stronger than
anything she had here.

`[C] The sources give her a choice between marriage and war, and a death that is about fidelity to
her murdered husband. The series keeps the death and changes the reasoning, and flags it: this is
the biggest single interpretive liberty in Book One. What is preserved is the outcome — she dies by
fire, of her own choosing, and the city is not inherited by anyone.`

What she did instead was older and colder than anything in the legend. She accepted. She named a
date. She had the pyre built in the open, told the assembled city and the chief's envoys that she
would go to her husband as they had all so kindly wished, and made the chiefs witnesses to a death
they had caused.

A death like that is a debt. Blood-guilt binds. The men who had demanded her could not now demand
the land as well, and the council — who had all voted against her three days earlier — inherited a
freehold and a martyr in the same afternoon.

Elishat, who was nineteen, spliced the ropes that bound the pyre, because she was the one who could
do it neatly and someone had asked her to.

## 19–22. The hide

After that, three days in which nobody governed anything.

Then, because it was obvious and because they were frightened of ever again depending on one person,
they split her authority. Two officers, elected, annual, with the council above them and the
priesthoods beside them.

`[B] The suffeteship almost certainly evolved rather than being invented in an afternoon. The series
compresses, and tells the audience it is compressing. What matters is the motive: the office exists
because of a fear of individuals, and in four hundred years that fear will be a court with a
hundred and four judges in it, and it will destroy the best men Carthage produces.`

Twenty years later, Elishat was a grandmother with bad hands and a rope business, and she stood at
the back of a crowd on the hill and listened to a young priest — the Cyprus priest's grandson, with
the office his family had bought on a beach — tell the story of the founding to a hundred children.

He told them that the queen had asked the Libyans for as much land as an ox-hide would cover. He
told them that she had cut the hide into a thread as fine as a hair, and rung the whole hill with it,
and that the Libyans, being simple, had been amazed.

He told them that she had died rather than marry a barbarian king, for love of her murdered husband.

The children were delighted. It is a very good story. It has a clever woman, a stupid enemy, and a
noble death, and it is better in every particular than eleven days of arguing about grazing rights
and a political suicide conducted for reasons of property law.

Elishat's son, standing beside her, waited for her to say something.

She did not. On the way home she stopped at the wall, took down the cord, and tied the second knot,
and handed it to him.

> *[Chapter 22 is the thesis of the series: the distance between what happened and what gets told,
> and the fact that the distance always grows in the direction that is most useful. Every Book from
> here on contains one scene in which the founding is retold, worse. By Book Five, Hannibal has been
> raised on a version of his city's origin that is almost entirely false, and it is one of the things
> that makes him possible.]*

# BOOK TWO — THE MERCHANT SEA
*c. 750–264 BC · chapters 23–60 · **Season 1**, second half*

> *Here I must go quickly, because five hundred years is a great deal of time and because most of
> what happened in it was prosperity, and prosperity is not a story. Let me put it as a merchant
> would: for three hundred years, everything they touched made money, and each thing that made money
> obliged them to defend it, and each defence obliged them to buy an army, and each army had to be
> paid out of something new.*

## 23–25. Cargo

The city grew the way a ledger grows.

Tin came down the Atlantic from an island nobody would name. Silver came out of Iberia in such
quantity that the ships had to be rebuilt to carry it; there is a story — the series shows it, as a
sailor's boast, and lets it be a boast — that on the first voyages they made the anchors out of
silver because there was nothing else to weight the ships with. Ivory and ostrich shells and gold
came up the caravan roads from the south. Grain came off Sardinia and Sicily. Salt fish and garum
went out; and dyed cloth, and perfume, and terracotta faces with wild grins on them to keep the evil
eye off a merchant's house, mass-produced in moulds, in the tens of thousands, cheap.

Carthage did not make much that anyone would call art. It made **things people wanted, at a price**,
and it moved them, and it took a cut of everything that passed through the western sea.

By 650 BC there were Carthaginians on Ibiza and the network had quietly changed owners. The colonies
of Tyre were now the clients of Tyre's colony. The tithe still went east every year, because it
always had.

## 26–29. Mago

The trouble with a trading empire is that it is a set of doors, and every door needs a man at it.

Greeks were coming west in numbers now — Phocaeans founding Massalia around 600 BC, Greek ships in
Sardinian and Corsican and Sicilian water, Greek colonies looking for the same harbours, the same
timber, the same grain. Two civilisations with identical business models arrived at the same coast,
and what followed was not a clash of cultures. It was a competition for shelf space, conducted with
ships.

Mago is the man who industrialised the response. `[A] Mago I, from around 550 BC, founder of the
house that ran Carthage for two centuries.` What he did, as far as we can tell, was three things:
he made the army a permanent professional institution rather than a levy of citizens; he made his
own family's command of it permanent; and he stopped paying the rent.

The series gives the last of these a chapter of its own, and gives it to a Libyan.

Ayzid's family had received the payment for four generations. It was not, by then, a large sum
relative to what Carthage was worth; it was a formality; it was, in his father's phrase, *the thing
that keeps them ours*. In the year the series dates it, the payment simply did not come. Ayzid rode
in to ask. He was received courteously by a clerk who explained, with genuine kindness, that the
arrangement had been reviewed.

Within two generations the Bagradas valley was a landscape of Carthaginian estates with Libyan
tenants on them, and the finest agricultural science in the world was being applied to land whose
owners now worked it for a share.

> *[This is the transformation the series is about. Not a battle. A clerk.]*

## 30–32. Alalia, and a line on the water

Off Corsica, somewhere between 540 and 535 BC, sixty Phocaean ships met a hundred and twenty
Carthaginian and Etruscan ones.

The Greeks won. They held the water, and they broke the allied line, and two-thirds of their fleet
was wrecked or crippled doing it, and they left Corsica within the year because they no longer had
the ships to hold it.

`[C] Herodotus calls it a "Cadmean victory" — a win that ruins the winner. The details are thin.`

The series uses it as the first full naval sequence and as the first statement of the central
military idea: that **a battle is an event and a war is an economy**, and Carthage in this period is
better at economies than anyone alive. Etruria took Corsica. Carthage took Sardinia, which had the
grain and the silver, and installed garrisons, and on Sardinia a man carved a stone that would still
be readable two and a half thousand years later, and probably mentions a king called Pygmalion, and
certainly did not mean to be important.

In 509 BC — the year, as it happens, that a small inland Italian city threw out its kings —
Carthaginian officials signed a treaty with Rome.

The terms were: Roman ships may not sail west past a certain headland. Roman merchants in
Carthaginian territory must trade under state supervision. Romans wrecked on Carthaginian shores get
five days and repairs and nothing else. In return, Carthage will not build forts in Latium and will
not molest the coastal towns Rome protects.

It is an entirely one-sided document, and the Carthaginian officials who drafted it did so in an
afternoon, and the series shows them doing it in an afternoon, and lets the audience sit with that
for the rest of the show.

## 33–35. Himera

In 480 BC, Carthage sent an army to Sicily, and Gelon of Syracuse destroyed it.

The series stages Himera as the first true catastrophe: three chapters, the last of them almost
silent. Hamilcar — the Magonid, grandson of Mago — spent the battle at the altars behind the camp,
burning offerings one after another, because the fleet had been fired and the cavalry had not come
and the thing was slipping, and the more it slipped the more he burned.

`[C] Herodotus says he walked into the fire himself when he saw the rout. Others say he was killed.
The series uses Herodotus' version and shoots it from a distance, through smoke, so that the audience
cannot be certain either — which is exactly the epistemic position of everyone who has ever written
about it.`

What matters is what Carthage did next, and it did not do what a kingdom would have done.

It paid the indemnity — two thousand talents — and it went home, and then it turned on the family
that had lost the army. Not with a purge. With an institution.

They created a court. A hundred and four judges, appointed for life, drawn from the great houses,
whose function was to examine generals: their conduct, their accounts, their decisions, their
ambitions. From 480 BC onward, a Carthaginian commander went to war knowing that if he lost he would
be tried, and if he won too well he would be watched.

> *Understand this and you understand everything that comes later. A Roman consul who lost an army
> could be consul again in ten years; Varro, who lost at Cannae, was thanked by the Senate for not
> despairing of the republic. A Carthaginian general who lost an army came home to a court, and
> sometimes to a cross. It is not that the Carthaginians were crueller. It is that the Romans were
> a state that expected to lose sometimes, and Carthage was a business that could not afford to.*

## 36–41. The chariot of the gods

Cut off from Sicily, Carthage went where nobody could follow: down the Atlantic coast of Africa,
and north into the fog.

Hanno took sixty ships. The number of people aboard is given as thirty thousand, which is certainly
wrong and is probably a scribe's error or a boast, and the series says so and then uses a plausible
figure and moves on.

Bodmelqart — Bodo, the rope family's fourth generation, a shipwright with a bad back and a gift for
calculating water — signed on because the pay was absurd and because he was thirty and had never
been anywhere.

What followed is the only piece of Carthaginian literature that survives, and it is four pages long,
and it survives in Greek.

They planted settlements along the Moroccan coast. They passed a river full of crocodiles and
hippopotami. They coasted a shore where, at night, the land was full of fires and the sound of pipes
and drums and shouting, and no one came out to them and they did not go in. They passed a mountain
burning from its summit, and called it the Chariot of the Gods, and were frightened. Their
interpreters were from the Lixus and did not know any of the languages they now heard.

And at the end, on an island in a gulf, they found people covered in hair, who would not be taken.
The men escaped by climbing. Three of the women were caught, and fought so savagely that the
Carthaginians killed them, and skinned them, and took the skins home; and the skins hung in a temple
in Carthage for three hundred years, until a Roman army burned the temple.

The series films this without commentary and refuses — absolutely refuses — to tell the audience
what they were.

Bodo came home with his hearing damaged and a tin of dried unknown fruit and a piece of information
worth more than either, which he sold to a syndicate: where the water was, and how far apart.

On the same day, or near enough, another expedition was sailing north to the tin islands under a
man named Himilco, who came back and told everyone the northern sea was choked with weed, windless,
shallow, full of monsters, and not worth anyone's time. The series presents his report exactly as
he delivers it, in public, to an audience of rival merchants, and lets the audience work out for
themselves that he is lying.

## 42–47. The grandson

Seventy years is nothing to a family with records.

In 410 BC the Carthaginian Assembly gave an army to **Hannibal Mago**, whose grandfather had burned
at Himera, and who had been raised, the series proposes, in a house where that was the first thing
anyone told a visitor.

He took Selinus in nine days and killed everyone the army could catch. He took Himera — the town
itself, the actual place — and had three thousand prisoners brought to the ground where his
grandfather had died, and killed them there.

The series does not make this cathartic. It films it as work: tired men, a queue, a long afternoon,
an officer keeping a tally on a wax tablet because someone will want the number.

Then he went to Akragas and the siege took eight months and plague came into the lines, and Hannibal
Mago, who had waited seventy years for his grandfather, died of a disease in a tent without seeing
the city fall.

Himilco II finished it, and sacked Gela, and came back, and went out again, and in 398 Dionysius of
Syracuse smashed the Punic city of Motya with a new machine that threw bolts further than a man
could shoot — the first artillery in the history of the world, invented in a workshop in Syracuse by
engineers on a state salary, which is a thing the series pauses on, because Carthage had ten times
Syracuse's money and had never thought to do it.

And then plague again, in Himilco's own camp outside Syracuse, worse than the first: thousands dead
in the lines, the army rotting where it stood. He bought his own citizens' passage out and left the
mercenaries, which was legal and practical and unforgivable. He came home, walked through the city
in mourning, told the Assembly it was his fault and not the gods', and starved himself to death.

`[C] The suicide is reported and is plausible. The manner is not verifiable.`

And Carthage, having decided the plague was the anger of the Greek goddesses whose shrine its army
had looted, **imported Demeter and Kore into its own state religion**, built them a temple, and
appointed priests.

> *[Chapter 70 — the series' favourite fact. A civilisation looks at a catastrophe, concludes that
> its enemy's god was correct, and buys the god. It is absurd, and it is documented, and it is the
> purest possible expression of what Carthage was: a city that believed, genuinely and to the end,
> that everything could be acquired.]*

## 48–51. The Sacred Band

For forty years after that, Carthage did well in Sicily and gained nothing by it. Two victories over
Dionysius, an indemnity of a thousand talents, a line drawn on a map at the Halycus river, and then
the same line redrawn, and redrawn again.

In this quiet stretch a retired general sat down on an estate in Cape Bon and wrote twenty-eight
books about farming: grafting, manuring, the drainage of heavy soils, when to plant vines on a north
slope, how to choose an ox. His name was Mago, which was the commonest name in Carthage. He is the
best writer the city ever produced, and the only one Rome thought worth keeping. When Carthage
burned, the Roman Senate gave the libraries away to Numidian kings and had Mago's books — and only
Mago's books — translated into Latin by decree.

> *[Chapter 74 is a quiet chapter about manure, placed deliberately between two massacres.]*

Meanwhile the aristocracy had begun to notice that it no longer fought.

The answer was the Sacred Band: twenty-five hundred young men of the best families, the finest
equipment in the western Mediterranean, white shields, matched spears, drilled in the Greek manner
on the plain outside the walls where their mothers could watch.

Adonibaal — sixth generation of the rope family, nineteen, strong as a mule — applied, was not
eligible, and went along anyway as a baggage handler, because the pay was good.

At the river Crimissus in 339 BC they met Timoleon's Syracusans on a day when the weather broke.
Hail came down the valley into Carthaginian faces. The river rose while the army was crossing it.
The four-horse chariots bogged. The Sacred Band, which had crossed first because that was the post
of honour, formed up on the far bank in mud and hail with the rest of the army stranded on the wrong
side of a rising river — and held, and was destroyed.

Afterwards there was a field of white shields.

Carthage mourned the flower of its youth for one season and then, and this is the part the series
insists on, drew the correct managerial conclusion. Never again would the sons of good families
stand in the line. From now on, the city would hire.

> *It was the sensible decision. Every step of what follows is the sensible decision. That is the
> horror of it.*

## 52. Tyre is gone

In 332 BC, Alexander of Macedon came down the coast of Phoenicia and demanded to sacrifice in the
temple of Melqart on the island of Tyre. Tyre, correctly reading the demand for what it was,
refused.

He built a mole out to the island. It took seven months. When it was finished he took the city,
crucified two thousand men along the shore, and sold everyone else.

The news came to Carthage on a grain ship. There were Carthaginian envoys in Tyre at the time; some
of them had been sheltering refugees. The city that had sent the colony out six hundred years before
was gone in an autumn.

The series gives the tithe ship one wordless page: loaded, blessed, and lying in the roads with
nowhere to go.

## 53–59. He burned his ships

Agathocles of Syracuse was a potter's son who had made himself tyrant, and in 311 BC a Carthaginian
army under Hamilcar son of Gisco beat him at the Himera river and shut him inside his own city with
a fleet across the harbour mouth.

Everyone understood what came next. It had happened eight times in two hundred years. The siege
would grind on, there would be plague or money trouble or both, and there would be a treaty on the
Halycus.

In August 310 BC, Agathocles ran sixty ships out through a gap in the blockade, was chased for three
days, and landed his whole army — perhaps fourteen thousand men — on the coast of Africa.

Then he burned the ships. All of them. In front of the army, with the crews watching, having first
told the soldiers that they were standing on the richest farmland in the world and that behind them
there was now nothing whatever.

`[A] He did it. It worked.`

What the series films is not the burning. It is Carthage seeing the smoke.

Three hundred years of absolute security ended in a morning. The estates of Cape Bon — the irrigated
orchards, the vineyards, the model farms out of Mago's books, the country houses of every man in the
Senate — were four days' march from an enemy army, and there was no army in Africa to stop it,
because every soldier Carthage owned was in Sicily.

They raised a levy of citizens. It was the first time in generations. Senators, merchants,
shipowners and the sons of the great houses took down armour that had hung in their halls since the
Crimissus, marched out, and were beaten by professionals.

And in the panic, the city did something the Greeks wrote down and the Carthaginians did not.

> *Diodorus says: five hundred children. Two hundred given up by the noble houses, and three hundred
> more offered voluntarily — because it had emerged that for years the great families had been buying
> poor children and substituting them for their own, and now the gods were angry and the debt had to
> be paid honestly.*
>
> *I was not there. Diodorus was not there either. He wrote three hundred years afterwards, in
> Sicily, in Greek, for readers who had been told since childhood what Carthaginians were.*

The series films chapter 56 in fragments, from outside: a precinct wall, a crowd, smoke, a priest's
voice, and two Carthaginians arguing bitterly on a stairway — one saying the number is a Greek lie
and the rite is rare and by vow only, the other saying he has just come from there. The audience
does not get to resolve it. Nobody alive gets to resolve it.

`[C] This is the tophet question. See the dossier, Part Four. The series' position is that something
real is being exaggerated by people with a motive, and that the exaggeration has become the only
thing most of the world now knows about a six-hundred-year-old civilisation.`

The Libyans went over to Agathocles in numbers — Zamar of the tent of Iyarut among them, and the
series gives him one line about why, and it is arithmetic, not treason. Agathocles murdered his
Libyan ally when he no longer needed him, then invited Ophellas of Cyrene to share the conquest, met
him with an embrace, killed him, and took his army.

And in Carthage, in 308 BC, with the Greeks still on African soil, a Carthaginian general named
Bomilcar marched his own troops into the city to make himself tyrant.

Three chapters. It is the only successful defence in the series conducted entirely by civilians.
They fought him street by street, from the rooftops, in a city built six storeys high with alleys
wide enough for two men — and they beat him, and crucified him in the marketplace, where he is said
to have denounced them from the cross with considerable accuracy about their treatment of their own
generals.

> *[The audience has now watched the Hundred and Four's nightmare come true once. Everything that
> court does to Hamilcar Barca and to Hannibal in Books Four and Five is, from its own point of view,
> entirely justified by chapter 58.]*

Agathocles' African adventure ended in farce and horror. Beaten in the field and out of money, he
slipped away by sea and left his army behind — and his two sons with it. The army killed the sons.
The peace of 306 BC restored the old line exactly.

Four years, tens of thousands dead, the chora burned, the Libyans in revolt, a coup in the streets,
and at the end the same line on the same map.

## 60. The wrestling-ground

When Pyrrhus of Epirus came west a generation later, Carthage and Rome signed a treaty against him:
mutual aid, Carthaginian ships to carry Roman troops, each state paying its own men. It is the last
document the two of them ever signed as friends.

Pyrrhus beat the Romans twice at a price he could not afford, crossed to Sicily, took Eryx and
Panormus, failed at the walls of Lilybaeum, exhausted his welcome, and sailed away.

Plutarch says that as the Sicilian coast went under the horizon he looked back and said: *what a
wrestling-ground we are leaving, my friends, for the Romans and the Carthaginians.*

`[C] Almost certainly composed after the fact. The series uses it with the camera on the sailors
rather than on Pyrrhus — because a line like that is exactly the kind of thing that gets attached to
a famous man afterwards, and the series has spent ninety-six chapters teaching the audience how that
happens.`

End of Book Two, and of Season One.

# BOOK THREE — THE SEA BETWEEN
*264–241 BC · chapters 61–92 · the First Punic War · **Season 2**, first half*

## 61–65. Both letters

The war began over a town held by bandits.

The Mamertines were Campanian mercenaries who had been hired by Syracuse, and who had taken the city
of Messana instead, killed the men, married the widows, and spent twenty years living off the
countryside. In 265 BC, Hiero of Syracuse finally moved to finish them, and the Mamertines — with no
shame at all, because shame was not among their assets — wrote to Carthage and to Rome on the same
day.

The series films both councils.

Carthage says yes quickly, because Messana commands the strait, and a Carthaginian garrison is in
the citadel within the month.

Rome argues for a season. The Senate does not want it: to fight for the Mamertines is to endorse
exactly the crime Rome had recently punished with executions in its own army. The Assembly votes for
it anyway, because the consuls tell them there is money in Sicily, and there is.

The garrison commander in Messana, a Carthaginian named Hanno, is talked out of the citadel by the
Mamertines without a fight — bloodlessly, sensibly, avoiding a battle he could not win.

He is crucified for it.

> *[Chapter 101. One page. Two lines of dialogue. The audience has been watching this court since
> chapter 35, and this is where it starts killing people the audience likes.]*

## 66–69. Agrigentum

The first great siege of the war was a Roman siege of a Greek city that Carthage was defending.

It lasted seven months. Both armies starved; both camps had plague; a Carthaginian relief force
under Hanno came and was beaten; the garrison escaped at night, competently, leaving the civilians.
And the Romans went in and sold twenty-five thousand people.

Four chapters, built around one family on a beach: a father with a good coat, a wife, three
children, and a Roman contractor with a wax tablet, sorting them by price.

> *This is the most important thing that happened in the first ten years of the war, and no battle
> report mentions it. Every Greek city in Sicily now knew two facts. The Carthaginians would leave
> you to hold a wall you could not hold. The Romans would sell you afterwards. Those two facts
> governed the loyalties of the island for the rest of the century, and nobody on either side ever
> wrote them down.*

## 70–76. The raven, and the sea

Carthage had been the first sea power in the world for four hundred years. Rome had, at the start of
this war, effectively no navy.

In 260 BC the Romans copied a wrecked Carthaginian quinquereme — a hull built, as Punic hulls were,
from numbered prefabricated parts, so that it could be taken apart and read like a set of
instructions — and built a fleet in sixty days.

`[C] The sixty days is Polybius and is probably compressed. The prefabrication is archaeologically
attested, from the Marsala wreck. The series uses both and makes the point that Carthage's greatest
industrial secret was legible to anyone who found one broken ship.`

Carthaginian officers laughed at the new Roman fleet. Milkat, a Carthaginian rower of the rope
family's ninth generation, did not laugh, because he had seen what they had bolted to the bows.

The corvus was a boarding bridge eleven metres long with an iron spike under the end. When the ships
closed, it dropped. It turned a sea battle — the most technical form of warfare in the ancient
world, requiring generations of seamanship — into a fight between infantry on a wooden platform,
which was the one thing on earth Rome was best at.

At Mylae they took fifty ships.

At Cape Ecnomus in 256 BC, some six hundred and eighty warships and perhaps a quarter of a million
men met in the largest naval engagement that has ever been fought, and the series films four
chapters of it almost entirely from below deck, where nobody can see anything: the timing call, the
change of stroke, the noise coming through the hull, the water, and a man at an oar trying to work
out from the sounds whether his side is winning.

## 77–83. Regulus, and the storm

Rome landed in Africa.

Marcus Atilius Regulus gets three chapters of proper introduction, because the series' rule is that
Rome must always have at least one man in it whom the audience would trust with their money. He is
a small farmer with a modest estate, elected consul twice, famously frugal, entirely without
imagination, and completely certain of the moral order of the universe.

He beat the Carthaginians at Adys, took Tunis, and sat down sixteen kilometres from the walls of
Carthage. And Carthage sued for peace.

And Regulus offered terms so severe that no state could have accepted them, and he offered them
kindly, and the series makes that the point of chapter 80. He is not gloating. He genuinely cannot
imagine why a beaten people would refuse to be beaten. He is the mirror of every Carthaginian
senator who has ever assumed a problem could be closed with a payment.

Carthage refused, and hired a Spartan.

Xanthippus was a professional soldier looking for work. He arrived, watched the Carthaginian army
drill, and told them in public that they had been beaten by their own commanders and not by the
Romans: they had elephants and would not use them in the open; they had the best cavalry in the
world and were fighting in the hills; they had a phalanx and no idea how to time it.

They gave him the army. In one afternoon on the plain outside Tunis he destroyed Regulus, killed
most of his army, and took him prisoner.

Then he collected his fee and went home, because he was a professional, and the series lets him
leave in the middle of a chapter without a farewell scene.

`[C] The Roman legend that Regulus was later sent to Rome on parole, argued against his own exchange,
returned voluntarily, and was tortured to death, is late, patriotic and probably invented. The series
shows it being invented: a Roman family telling it at a dinner forty years later, in Book Five.`

And then, sailing home with the survivors, the Roman fleet met a storm off Camarina and lost three
hundred and eighty-four ships out of four hundred and sixty-four.

Perhaps a hundred thousand men. No enemy. Weather.

Chapter 122 has eleven pages without dialogue, and the closing image is Roman families on a dock in
summer, being told by a clerk with a list that there is no list.

## 84–86. Drepana

Rome built another fleet, and lost most of that one too, and built another.

Carthage won its greatest naval victory at Drepana in 249 BC, when Adherbal caught a Roman fleet in
a harbour mouth and destroyed it. Before the battle the consul, Publius Claudius Pulcher, was told
the sacred chickens would not eat, which was the worst possible omen, and said: *then let them
drink*, and threw them over the side.

`[C] Obviously improved in the telling. Kept, because Rome told this story about itself as a warning
against impiety, and the series wants the audience to see a Roman disaster explained by Romans as a
religious failure rather than a naval one.`

After Drepana and Phintias, Carthage had swept Rome from the sea. And having won, the Senate at
Carthage reduced the naval budget, laid up ships, and cut the standing subsidy to the army in
Sicily, because the war appeared to be over and the expenditure was enormous.

It was, on the evidence available, a reasonable decision.

## 87–92. Eryx

In 247 BC the command in Sicily was given to a man of about thirty named Hamilcar, of a good but
not first-rank house, with a family nickname — *Baraq*, lightning — that his descendants would make
into one of the most famous words in the ancient world.

In the same season, in Carthage, his first son was born. The series has the two pieces of news
arrive in the same dispatch bag, and has Hamilcar read the military one first.

What he had was about fifteen thousand men, no reliable pay, no reinforcement, a government that
regarded the Sicilian war as a closed file, and the western tip of an island.

What he did with it, for six years, was refuse to be found.

He took Heircte, a flat-topped mountain above Panormus with water on it and a cove beneath it, and
from there he raided the coast of Italy — burning, taking cattle, taking people, keeping two Roman
consular armies committed and paying for his own war out of what he stole. When Heircte became
untenable he moved, by night, through Roman-held country, and seized Mount Eryx, with a Roman
garrison above him on the summit and another Roman army below him on the plain, and held a position
between two enemies for two more years.

He was never defeated. He was also, in any sense that mattered to the outcome of the war, entirely
irrelevant, and he knew it, and that is what Book Three is finally about.

> *He said a thing to his officers on Eryx which I had from a man who had it from his son, so treat
> it as you please. He said: they do not have to beat me. They only have to keep being there.*

In 241 BC, Rome built one more fleet — privately financed, by loans from citizens, on the security
of the republic — and put it to sea in winter with trained crews, and caught the Carthaginian relief
convoy off the Aegates Islands. The convoy was loaded with grain for Eryx and its crews were scratch.
It was destroyed in an hour.

Milkat's last chapter. He is forty, he has been at sea since he was sixteen, he is in the water for
a long time, and the chapter does not tell the audience whether he is picked up.

And the Senate at Carthage, having lost the fleet and with it the ability to supply an undefeated
army, sent Hamilcar Barca full authority to make peace on the best terms he could get.

He got: evacuate Sicily; return all prisoners; pay three thousand two hundred talents over ten
years. Carthage lost the first province of the Roman Empire and twenty-three years and, by any
reasonable estimate, a hundred thousand men, at sea and on land and in storms.

Hamilcar signed. Then he was ordered to disarm his own army and hand it over for transport home,
and he refused to do it personally, and resigned the command, and gave the job to another officer.

`[A] He did resign rather than conduct the disarmament. The series treats it as the most important
decision he ever made, because of what it caused.`

The officer's name was Gisco, and he was a decent, careful man, and he shipped the army home in
small batches so that Carthage could pay each batch off before the next arrived.

Carthage did not pay them off. Carthage concentrated all of them in one place instead, at Sicca,
because keeping twenty thousand armed unpaid men inside a city was obviously unwise, and because
the treasury was empty and the indemnity's first instalment was due.

End of Book Three.

# BOOK FOUR — TRUCELESS
*241–237 BC · chapters 93–117 · the bill · **Season 2**, second half*

## 93–99. The wages

Twenty thousand men. Iberians, Libyans, Gauls, Ligurians, Greeks, Balearic slingers, Campanians,
runaway slaves. Between them perhaps fifteen languages and no common one. Undefeated, unpaid, and
sitting in a town in the interior with nothing to do.

The series spends eight chapters here, because this is the hinge of the entire saga and because it
is a *procurement failure* before it is anything else.

Carthage sent negotiators who offered less than was owed, and then a little more, and then a little
more — which taught the army that the number was not fixed and that patience paid. Carthage sent
minor officials because the senior men did not want the job. Carthage at last sent Gisco, who was
the one Carthaginian the soldiers trusted, and sent him too late and with too little money, and he
spent it as fairly as he could, which meant that the men who got paid went quiet and the men who did
not got louder.

And into that gap came three men.

**Spendius** was a runaway Roman slave. If there was a settlement, he would be handed back under the
treaty, and he would be crucified, and he knew it with total clarity. Therefore there could not be a
settlement. He is the coldest and most rational character in the series, and the audience understands
him completely.

**Autaritus** was a Gaul who had learned Punic, which made him the man through whom the army heard
everything. He did not need to lie often. He needed to translate with emphasis.

**Mathos** was a Libyan, and the series gives him the case for the prosecution.

He is not a bandit and not a fanatic. He is a man from a village in the Bagradas that has paid
tribute for two hundred years; that had its tribute doubled during the war, as the law allowed; that
has been emptied of men twice by the levy; and that farms land that was its own within living
memory and is now an estate. He says all of this once, in chapter 99, to Gisco, quietly, in a tent,
in good Punic, and Gisco — who is an honest man — has no answer that is not a promise about the
future, and Mathos says: *you have said that to my family for six generations.*

The rising, when it came, came with a demand that the men could not walk back from: they seized
Gisco and his staff. After that there was no negotiated peace available to anybody.

## 100–105. Africa rises

Seventy thousand Libyans joined. Women in the villages gave up their jewellery to pay the rebel
army, which the Carthaginians found incomprehensible and the series does not.

Utica and Hippacritae — Phoenician cities older than Carthage — massacred their Carthaginian
garrisons and joined the rebellion. Utica offered itself to Rome.

And Rome refused.

> *[Chapter 143. Rome refuses, correctly and honourably, on the grounds that there is a treaty. The
> audience should be unsettled by how much they like Rome in this chapter, because in fourteen
> chapters' time Rome will take Sardinia on a pretext, and the series wants both of those things to
> be true at once.]*

Hanno the Great commanded first, and won a battle, and lost the camp afterwards because his men went
looting, which is what happens when troops are paid in plunder.

So Carthage, with the rebels holding the isthmus and the city effectively under siege, recalled
Hamilcar Barca and gave him an army of ten thousand.

At the Bagradas he crossed a river everyone believed impassable, let the enemy come at him in
column, and then reversed his front — the elephants and the light troops pulling back through the
line by design, so that what looked like a rout turned into a formed army facing the pursuers at
close range. Eight thousand dead.

It is the first Barcid battle in the series, and it is used against Carthage's own soldiers.

Then Naravas rode in.

He was a Numidian chief with two thousand horse, nominally on the rebel side. He rode into the
Carthaginian camp with a handful of companions, unarmed, asked for Hamilcar, and said that he had
admired his father, and that he would bring his cavalry over.

Hamilcar promised him his daughter.

`[A] All of this is in Polybius.`

Behind Naravas rode Iyarut the younger. His cousin Massiv was, that same week, with Mathos.

## 106–112. Seven hundred

Hamilcar's policy with prisoners was, at first, mercy as a weapon: enlist with me, or go home
unharmed on your oath. It worked. Men began slipping away from the rebel camp in tens and twenties.

So Spendius and Autaritus destroyed the possibility of mercy, on purpose, in a single afternoon.

They took Gisco and the seven hundred Carthaginian prisoners out in front of the whole army. They
cut off their hands. They cut off more than that. They broke their legs and threw them, still alive,
into a pit.

And then, because the thing had to be irreversible, they put it to the assembled army as a vote, and
they had already arranged that anyone who spoke against it would be stoned as a traitor, and it
passed, and every man there was now complicit.

`[A] Polybius, and he is not a writer who enjoys this kind of thing.`

Hamilcar answered by having prisoners trampled by elephants.

After that neither side took prisoners for two years. The war has no name in Punic that we know of;
Polybius calls it *the truceless war*, and says he knows of no conflict in history so savage.

Three chapters later comes the Saw: a defile in the hills where Hamilcar penned some forty thousand
men behind field works and simply waited. They ate the horses. They ate the pack animals. They ate
their slaves. When the leaders came out under a flag to negotiate, he arrested them, and when the
army rushed the works in fury, he killed it.

Spendius was crucified. Mathos was taken at Leptis Parva and brought to Carthage and given to the
crowd.

> *[Chapters 155–156 are about the crowd. Shopkeepers, mothers, boys. The camera stays with a woman
> who has lost two sons in the fighting and who is entirely, humanly, justified, and the sequence is
> filmed so that the audience cannot look away from what justification produces.]*

## 113–114. Sardinia

And then, while Carthage was on its knees, the mercenary garrison of Sardinia — which had mutinied
too — was driven off the island by the Sardinians themselves, and Carthage prepared an expedition to
take back its own province.

Rome announced that this expedition was an act of war against Rome.

There was no legal basis whatsoever. Sardinia was Carthaginian by every treaty both states had ever
signed. Rome said it anyway, and Carthage — which had just lost a hundred thousand men in
twenty-three years, and forty thousand more in three, and could not have put ten thousand men in the
field — paid Sardinia and Corsica, and twelve hundred talents on top.

> *Polybius, who was a Greek living in Rome and writing for Romans, and who admired them, wrote that
> this was the single greatest cause of the war that followed. He did not think it was a close call.*

The series films the news arriving at Hamilcar's house. He is told; he asks the man to repeat the
figure; he says thank you; he goes on with what he was doing.

His eldest son is six years old and is in the room.

## 115–117. The altar

`[A] What follows is from Polybius, who had it from men who had it from Hannibal himself, who told
the story decades later to Antiochus III of Syria in order to convince him that he could be trusted
never to make peace with Rome. Consider, when you watch it, that the only version we have is a
version Hannibal chose to tell for a political purpose.`

In 237 BC, Hamilcar Barca obtained from the Assembly — by spending money, by reminding them who had
saved the city, and by promising that the indemnity would be paid out of Iberian silver — a command
in Spain.

Before he sailed he sacrificed.

And his son, who was nine, asked to be taken along.

The series films it plainly. A boy asking his father for something. A pause that goes on slightly
too long. Then the father takes him by the hand, walks him to the altar where the victim is lying
open, puts the boy's hand on the meat, and asks him to swear that he will never be a friend of Rome.

The boy swears. He would have sworn to anything; he has been asking for months; he is nine.

Chapter 160 then does one further thing, and it is the most important editorial decision in the
series. It cuts, without comment, to the room where four senior officers have been waiting outside,
and one of them says to another: *did he do it?* And the other says: *of course he did it.* And they
go back to work, because the army is marching in the morning and the policy was decided a year ago
in a different city by men who have never met this child.

> *The oath is real. I heard him refer to it myself, twice. What I could never determine, in thirty
> years, is whether it was the cause of his life or the excuse for it — and I do not believe he
> could have told you either.*

They marched to Spain overland, along the African coast to the Straits, because Carthage could not
spare enough ships to move an army. Bostar, the quartermaster — the rope family's tenth generation,
twenty-four years old and good with numbers — walked the whole way counting.

End of Book Four, and of Season Two.

# BOOK FIVE — SILVER
*237–218 BC · chapters 118–136 · **Season 3**, first half*

## 118–128. Silver

What Hamilcar built in Iberia over nine years was not a province. It was a private state.

He took the silver mines of the south-east, and the silver paid the army, and the army was recruited
locally and was loyal to him. He made treaties with Iberian chiefs in his own name, took their sons
as hostages and their daughters as brides for his officers, founded a fortress city called Akra
Leuke, and shipped enough bullion home each year to keep the Assembly voting for him and Hanno the
Great writing letters nobody read.

Hannibal grew up in this. The series is careful about what that means.

He grew up in a camp, not a city; among six languages; among hostages his own age — the sons of
chiefs, who were his companions and his friends and also the security on his family's debts, which
he understood by the time he was fourteen. He learned Greek from a Spartan called Sosylos, who had
been hired because Hamilcar wanted his sons to be able to read what the Greeks wrote about them.

> *He was not a charming boy. He was watchful, and very hard to please, and quicker than the adults
> around him, and he knew it, which is not an attractive quality at fifteen. What I remember is that
> he learned languages the way other boys collect knives — not for the beauty of them; because he
> wanted to know what people were saying when they thought he could not understand.*

In 228 BC Hamilcar was caught in the open by an Iberian force — the Oretani, probably — and died
covering his sons' retreat across a river. He drowned, according to one tradition. He was killed
after the enemy sent blazing ox-carts into his line, according to another.

`[C] The series shows both in one image: a man going under brown water while a burning cart rolls
through the shallows behind him. It does not explain.`

Hannibal was nineteen and on the far bank.

## 129–136. Hasdrubal, the treaty, and Saguntum

Hamilcar's son-in-law Hasdrubal — called the Fair — took the command, was elected to it by the army
and confirmed at Carthage, and ruled Iberia for seven years without fighting a major battle.

He is the most underrated man in the story and the series treats him as the road not taken. He
married an Iberian princess. He made the chiefs partners rather than subjects; Polybius says they
acclaimed him their supreme commander, which is a remarkable thing for a foreign occupier. He built
a capital — New Carthage, on a harbour that had no equal in the western sea, with an arsenal and a
palace and the silver mines a day's ride inland. He bought where he could and fought where he had
to, and he made more money in seven years than Hamilcar had made in nine.

And around 226 BC he received Roman envoys who were worried about Carthaginian expansion, and signed
an agreement that Carthaginian arms would not cross the river Ebro.

`[C] Everything about this treaty is disputed. Whether Carthage ratified it. Whether it bound
Hasdrubal's successors. Whether it contained a clause protecting Rome's friends south of the line.
The series' position: it was deliberately vague, both parties knew it was vague, and each signed
believing the vagueness was in its own favour. Two chapters later, a Roman envoy and a Carthaginian
officer give flatly contradictory accounts of the same document, and the series never adjudicates.`

In 221 BC, Hasdrubal was stabbed to death in a corridor by a Celtic servant whose master he had
executed, and who was taken alive, and who — Polybius says — was tortured with such severity that
his expression was one of laughter, and who died without a sound.

The army acclaimed Hannibal, who was twenty-six. Carthage confirmed him. Hanno the Great stood up in
the Senate and said that a young man who had been raised in a camp by a father with a grudge should
not be given the largest army the republic possessed, and that the Barcids had made Spain into a
private kingdom, and that this would end in a war the city could not afford.

He was voted down, and he was right.

Hannibal spent two seasons doing exactly what a competent young general should do: subduing the
interior, taking Althia, storming Helmantice, and then — on the way home, ambushed by a coalition
under the Carpetani — fighting the battle at the Tagus that tells the audience who he is.

He does not fight for the crossing. He pulls back from the river at night, lets the enemy start
across in the morning in a body, and then puts forty elephants along the bank to walk down anyone
who reaches it and sends the cavalry into the water to kill men who are chest-deep and cannot use a
shield.

The series films him deciding it the evening before, step by step, out loud, to Sosylos, who is
writing it down. The audience gets the whole plan. Then they watch it happen exactly as described,
and the horror is in the exactness.

Then Saguntum.

Saguntum was a city south of the Ebro — on Hannibal's side of the line — which had made itself a
friend of Rome and had begun, with Roman encouragement, to interfere with its neighbours who were
friends of Carthage. Whether it was covered by the treaty is the question the whole war turns on,
and it was never answered, because both sides preferred the ambiguity until the moment neither did.

He besieged it for eight months. The series gives it one chapter, because a siege is not a story, it
is an erosion: mines and counter-mines, a wall breached and rebuilt, a truce that fails, hunger,
and at the end a sack.

He sent the plunder to Carthage and distributed it, which bought the Senate.

In the spring of 218 BC a Roman embassy came to Carthage, and the head of it, Quintus Fabius
Maximus, refused to argue the legal case at all. He gathered a fold of his toga and said that he
carried peace and war in it, and that they might take whichever they preferred.

And the Senate of Carthage told him to choose.

And he let the fold fall and said: *then take war.*

And they shouted back, all of them, that they accepted it.

End of Book Five.

# BOOK SIX — THE ROAD
*218–216 BC · chapters 137–157 · **Season 3**, second half*

> *Everything up to here has been the ground. Now the battle.*

## 137–138. Leaving New Carthage

The first thing he did was not military.

He took the Iberian troops who were to garrison Africa and shipped them to Africa, and took the
African troops who were to garrison Iberia and shipped them to Iberia. Every soldier in his empire
was now a thousand miles from his own village, and every village was within reach of somebody else's
garrison.

Sosylos asked him, in Greek, whether he did not think this was a cold way to treat men who had
volunteered.

He said: *they did not volunteer. Their fathers signed.*

He left his brother Hasdrubal in Spain with an army he knew to be too small and told him so. He left
his wife, Imilce, of whom the sources tell us almost nothing — a name, a city, a doubtful son — and
the series gives her one chapter and no melodrama, and she is not mentioned again, because he never
mentioned her again either.

And in the late spring of 218 BC he marched north with something near fifty thousand foot, nine
thousand horse and thirty-seven elephants, and did not tell anyone where he was going.

## 139–145. The Alps

The Rhône was held. A Gallic army was drawn up on the eastern bank, and behind him, at the river's
mouth, a Roman consul had just landed an army with orders to stop him in Gaul.

He sent a detachment twenty-five miles upstream in the dark to cross on rafts, come down behind the
Gauls, and light a fire. When the Gauls turned to face the smoke, he crossed.

The elephants went over last, on rafts covered with earth and turf so that they would believe they
were walking on land. Two panicked and went into the water; they found the bottom and walked out,
which nobody had expected, and which the series films as the one moment of joy in the sequence.

Scipio's Romans arrived at the crossing three days later and found ashes.

Then he turned north, away from the coast road, away from the sea, away from every line of supply
Carthage had, and went up into the mountains in October.

Five chapters. The series' rule for the Alps sequence is that **nothing heroic happens**.

The Allobroges hold the high ground above a gorge and roll stones; men and animals go off the path,
and the path is a foot wide in places, and the army is strung out for miles and can neither
concentrate nor retreat. The local guides take payment and lead them wrong. A tribe comes out with
cattle and hostages and promises of friendship, and three days later ambushes the column in a defile
where the baggage is, and it is only because the elephants frighten the mountain horses that the
column is not cut in half.

Higher up there is no ambush, and it is worse. There is a rockfall across the path that the engineers
break up with fire — Livy says they poured vinegar on the hot stone, and the series shows a man
pouring sour wine on a rock and shows two soldiers watching him do it with expressions of complete
contempt, and lets the audience decide.

At the top there is a pause. Nine days of climbing, in snow, and the col is a flat cold place, and
he brings them up in groups and shows them Italy.

`[C] The pass is disputed. The series uses the Col de la Traversette, which is the only candidate
from which the Po plain is genuinely visible, and which has produced a churned peat layer with
horse-gut bacteria dated to around 218 BC. On screen it is never named.`

And then the descent, which kills more than the climb: new snow on old ice on a north slope, pack
animals going over, men sitting down in the snow and not getting up.

Safan is twenty. He came out of Carthage as a quartermaster's grandson who had never seen weather
like this and did not know such weather existed. He loses his mule, his brother, and his conviction
that anyone in the column knows what they are doing. His chapter ends with him carrying a sack of
barley he has no orders about, because it is the only thing left he can be sure is useful.

Fifteen days. He went into the mountains with perhaps forty-six thousand men. He came down with
twenty thousand foot and six thousand horse and a handful of elephants, into a plain full of Gauls
who hated Rome, and the first thing he did was attack a town and feed his army.

## 146–151. The river, the marsh and the lake

At the Ticinus, a cavalry skirmish, and a Roman consul is unhorsed and surrounded, and a
seventeen-year-old on a horse rides into the middle of it and drags his father out.

`[B] Polybius says it was his son. Another tradition says a Ligurian slave, and Polybius, who was a
client of that family, would say the son. The series uses the son and lets a slave stand in the
background of the frame, saying nothing.`

His name was Publius Cornelius Scipio, and the audience should not be told he is important.

At the Trebia in December, Hannibal sent the Numidians across the river at dawn to shoot arrows into
the Roman camp and run away. The Romans came out after them without eating, waded a river in flood
with ice in it, and formed up wet, on an empty stomach, in sleet. He had put his brother Mago and
two thousand men in the reed beds of a watercourse the night before, and they came up out of the
ground behind the Roman line.

The centre of the Roman army — ten thousand men — punched clean through the Carthaginian line and
marched away in good order, because they were extremely good soldiers, and that is the detail the
series keeps.

That winter killed almost all of the elephants.

In the spring he took his army through the flooded Arno marshes: four days and three nights in
water, sleeping on the bodies of dead pack animals when they could find one to lie on, the men
losing skin, the horses losing hooves. He rode the last surviving elephant because it was the only
thing high enough to keep him out of the water, and somewhere in those four days an infection took
his right eye, and there was no surgeon in the column who could do anything about it, and he lost it.

The series does not make this heroic either. There is a scene of a man holding a bowl.

And then Lake Trasimene, which is the most perfectly executed ambush in ancient warfare, and which
the series films as an atrocity.

He marched past a Roman army, in contempt, burning the countryside, to make Flaminius follow him
angry. Then he put his army along the wooded hills above a road that ran between the lake shore and
the heights, in the dark, and waited for fog.

The Roman column entered the defile in the morning mist in march order, four men abreast, with no
scouts out because they were in a hurry and could not see anything anyway. The Carthaginians came
down the hill on both sides at once.

There was no battle line. There was no room to form one. There was a road with water on one side,
and fifteen thousand men were killed in about three hours in a space where a man could not turn
around, and some thousands waded into the lake in armour and drowned, and some thousands were taken.
Flaminius was killed by a Gaul who knew who he was and had been looking for him.

Caeso Rutilius, twenty, of a smallholding in the Sabine hills, survived by lying in the shallows
under a floating corpse, breathing through the gap between a scabbard and its mouth, for as long as
it took. He is in the series for another twenty chapters and he never sleeps well again.

## 152. The Delayer

Rome appointed a dictator: Quintus Fabius Maximus, sixty-two years old, the man who had dropped the
fold of his toga in the Carthaginian Senate house four years before.

His strategy was this: never fight him. Shadow the army. Hold the high ground. Refuse every battle.
Cut off foragers. Burn the crops in front of him — Roman crops, Italian crops, the farms of Roman
citizens — so that the invader has nothing to eat, and let sixteen years do what no consul could do
in an afternoon.

It was the only strategy that could work, and it worked, and Rome hated him for it.

They called him the Delayer as an insult. They called him Hannibal's paedagogus — the slave who
walks a boy to school — because he followed the Carthaginian everywhere and did nothing. Farmers
whose land he burned came to Rome and shouted in the Forum. His own Master of Horse was given equal
command by a popular vote, which had never happened in the history of the republic, specifically as
a rebuke to him.

And when Hannibal got his army into the Falernian plain and Fabius corked the passes and had him —
genuinely had him, for one night, in a trap — Hannibal tied brushwood to the horns of two thousand
cattle, lit it, and drove them up a ridge at night; and the Romans guarding the pass went up the
ridge after the lights; and the Carthaginian army walked out through the empty pass in the dark.

Fabius, watching from his camp, suspected a trick and did not move, and was proved right, and was
mocked for that too.

> *[Chapter 196 is the series' portrait of political courage, and it is given to a Roman. It has to
> be. If the audience has come this far loving Carthage, this is the chapter that makes Rome
> impossible to hate.]*

Fabius' term expired. Rome, impatient, elected two consuls and raised the largest army it had ever
put in the field, and sent it to find Hannibal.

## 153–156. Cannae

Four chapters. One day. Eight hours.

**197 — The ground.** A dry summer plain in Apulia beside the Aufidus. A wind called the Volturnus
that gets up in the late morning and blows dust from south-east to north-west, into the faces of an
army facing that way. A supply depot at Cannae that Hannibal has already taken, so the Romans must
come to him. Two consuls, Varro and Paullus, alternating command daily, disagreeing about whether to
fight at all.

`[C] Livy blames Varro entirely; Polybius, patron-bound to Paullus' family, is not neutral either.
The series divides the responsibility and shows the disagreement as a genuine professional dispute
between two men who are each partly right.`

Eighty-six thousand Romans, in a formation deeper than usual, because the consuls intend to punch
straight through by weight of men. Fifty thousand Carthaginians, hungry, in a line that is not
straight.

**198 — The crescent.** The Gauls and Iberians in the centre are placed in a shallow arc bulging
towards the enemy. They are told to fight, and then to give ground, slowly, in order, and not to
break.

The series films chapter 154 from inside that centre. Not from the hill. From the second rank of a
Gallic company, where a man is being told by an officer he barely understands that he must walk
backwards all morning while the largest army in the world pushes into him, and that this is the plan.
They give ground. The arc flattens. The arc reverses and becomes a pocket. The Romans, feeling the
enemy give, push harder into it, and men in the Roman rear ranks start pressing forward because it
feels like winning, and the whole vast formation loses the ability to manoeuvre because it is too
tightly packed to turn.

Along the two inner edges of the pocket, the African veterans stand still in captured Roman armour
and wait.

**199 — The ride.** Hasdrubal — not the brother; a cavalry commander of the same name — takes the
heavy horse on the left, destroys the Roman cavalry opposite him, and then does the thing nobody
does. He does not pursue. He re-forms, rides the entire length of the battle behind the fighting,
takes the allied cavalry on the other wing from behind, drives it off, and comes back around into
the rear of the Roman infantry.

It requires perfect discipline and two hours, and it is the single most professional act of
horsemanship in the ancient world, and the series films it as a long, dusty, boring ride, because
that is what it was.

**200 — The hours.** The pocket closes.

Seventy thousand men are now inside an area smaller than the ground they started on. The Romans on
the outside are being killed; the Romans in the middle cannot reach anyone and cannot get out and
are being crushed by their own people. The killing takes about three hours and is done at arm's
length, with the short sword, by men who have to step over bodies to get to the next rank, and who
have to be relieved and rotated because they are too tired to lift their arms.

Four viewpoints. A Libyan veteran doing the work. A Roman tribune trying and failing to make a face
to the rear. Caeso Rutilius, who survived Trasimene, who is somewhere in the middle and cannot see
the sky for dust and cannot move his arms because of the press. And Hannibal, on foot, walking
behind his own line, who does almost nothing all day, because everything he was going to do he did
before dawn.

At least sixty-seven thousand Romans were killed or captured, in an afternoon, by hand.

Consul Paullus died in the line. Two former consuls died. Twenty-nine of forty-eight tribunes died.
About eighty men of senatorial rank died. It was, in proportion to the population that suffered it,
the worst day in the recorded history of the Roman state.

Varro escaped with a few thousand men and rode to Rome, expecting to be killed.

The Senate went out of the city to meet him, and thanked him publicly, in front of the people, for
not having despaired of the republic.

> *[That sentence is the end of chapter 156 and the beginning of chapter 157, and it is the moment
> the series has been building toward for two hundred chapters. Carthage crucified a garrison
> commander for saving his men without a battle. Rome thanked the man who lost sixty-seven thousand.
> Both cities were being entirely themselves. Only one of them could survive being itself.]*

## 157. Use it

That night, Maharbal, who commanded the Numidian cavalry, is said to have told him to ride for Rome
at once: *in five days you will dine on the Capitol. Send me ahead with the horse; let them know you
are coming before they know you have come.*

And Hannibal said he needed to think about it.

And Maharbal said: *the gods do not give all their gifts to one man. You know how to win a victory,
Hannibal. You do not know how to use one.*

`[C] Livy. Almost certainly composed. The series uses it and then spends the rest of the chapter
dismantling it.`

Because Hannibal's answer is correct, and the series lays it out, flatly, in a tent, with a map and a
supply return:

He has no siege train; it is in Spain, and the reason it is in Spain is that he crossed the Alps. He
has perhaps forty thousand men, of whom a third are Gauls who will go home for the harvest. Rome's
walls are the strongest in Italy and there are two legions of new levies inside them. A siege
requires a fleet to blockade the Tiber, and Carthage's fleet is a political question, not a military
one. And above all: **he did not come to Italy to take Rome. He came to take Rome's allies.** The
entire strategy, from the first day in New Carthage, was to prove to the Italians that Rome could be
beaten, so that Italy would come over and Rome would become one city among many again.

And it half worked. Capua came over. Most of the south came over. Syracuse came over. Philip of
Macedon came in on his side. Tarentum came over two years later.

And the Latin colonies did not. And central Italy did not. And Rome, having lost a fifth of its
adult male citizens in three years, raised two more legions from slaves bought at state expense and
promised freedom, refused to ransom the eight thousand prisoners Hannibal was holding — refused
even to discuss it — passed a law against public mourning, and kept fighting.

> *He could destroy any army they sent. He could not make them stop sending them. It took him,
> I think, four years to understand that this was not a problem he could solve on a battlefield,
> and he never in his life found any other kind of solution, and it destroyed him.*

End of Book Six, and of Season Three.

# BOOK SEVEN — THE LONG DEFEAT
*216–183 BC · chapters 158–184 · **Season 4**, first half*

## 158–162. Sixteen years

One chapter for a decade, and it is deliberately the fastest chapter in Book Five, because that is
what those years felt like: a great deal happening and nothing changing.

Capua defected and became his base and his hostage. Syracuse rose and Marcellus besieged it, and an
old man called Archimedes kept a Roman fleet off a wall for two years with cranes and mirrors and
engines, and when the city fell a soldier who did not know who he was killed him over a diagram in
the sand.

Rome besieged Capua. Hannibal marched on Rome itself to pull them off it — *Hannibal ad portas*,
the phrase Roman mothers would use to frighten children for six centuries — and camped within sight
of the walls, and the Romans did not lift the siege, and, according to a story Rome enjoyed telling,
the field his camp stood on came up for auction in the Forum during those days and sold at the full
pre-war price.

`[C] Almost certainly propaganda. The series shows it as propaganda: a Roman senator inventing it
over dinner, and the audience watching a legend being born in exactly the way they watched the
ox-hide legend born in chapter 21.`

Capua fell. Its ruling class was executed and the city ceased to exist as a political entity, which
told every other Italian city what defection cost.

In Spain, the two Scipio brothers — the consul from the Ticinus and his brother — were beaten and
killed within a month of each other in 211 BC, after their twenty thousand Celtiberian mercenaries
were bought away from them by Hasdrubal Barca for less than it would have cost to keep them.

And Carthage sent Hannibal, in sixteen years in Italy, one reinforcement.

`[A] One. Four thousand men and some elephants, to Locri, in 215 BC. Hanno the Great's faction
blocked the rest, and their argument was that Spain and Sardinia and Africa also had to be defended,
and that the money was finite, and that a man who had already taken his private army to Italy
without authorisation should not be sent a second one.`

> *[The series refuses to make this cowardice or spite. In chapter 152 of the manga Hanno is given a
> full page to make the argument, and the argument is: he is not fighting our war, he is fighting
> his father's war, and the day he wins it he will come home with sixty thousand men who love him and
> we will be a monarchy by autumn. The audience, who watched Bomilcar's coup in chapter 58, cannot
> simply dismiss this.]*

## 163–167. The other one

In 210 BC, Rome needed a commander for Spain and could not find one. Two armies had been destroyed
there and two proconsuls killed. Nobody of consular rank would take it.

A twenty-five-year-old put his name forward, who had held no qualifying office, whose father and
uncle had died there, and who had been at Ticinus and at Cannae. The Assembly elected him because
nobody else wanted it, and then — the series shows this — immediately regretted it and sent an
older man along to supervise.

What Scipio did in four years is the mirror of everything Hannibal had done, executed with a
politician's instincts that Hannibal did not have.

He took New Carthage in a single day, in 209 BC, by walking five hundred men across a lagoon at low
water on the north side — having, it is said, established from local fishermen that the water fell
there every evening, and having told his troops that Neptune had appeared to him in a dream and
would make a road.

`[A] He did it; the lagoon, the low water, and the divine dream are all reported. The series shows
him getting the tide table from a fisherman in the morning and the dream from himself in the
afternoon, in that order.`

In the city he took the Carthaginian treasury, the arsenals, and three hundred hostages — the sons
and daughters of every Iberian chief in the peninsula, the security on Barcid power for twenty
years.

He sent them home. Free. With escorts.

It cost him nothing, it destroyed Carthage's hold on Iberia, and it is exactly the method Hannibal
had used at Saguntum and in the Alps, done once more and better. A Spanish chief's wife asked him to
protect the girls from his soldiers, and he did, and had it announced, everywhere, in Iberian.

He beat Hasdrubal Barca at Baecula and did not destroy him. He crushed the last Carthaginian armies
at Ilipa in 206 BC by inverting his own line at dawn — putting his Romans on the wings and his
Spaniards in the centre, the opposite of what he had done every day for a week — and attacking
before the Carthaginians had eaten.

And when his own army mutinied over arrears of pay at Sucro, he crushed it, and executed the ringleaders, and made a speech about it, and was forgiven.

In the same years, in Italy, Marcellus — the Sword of Rome, the only Roman commander who would
willingly stand in front of Hannibal — was killed riding into a scouting ambush at the age of sixty,
and Hannibal had his body burned with honours and sent the ashes back.

## 168–169. His brother's head

In 207 BC, Hasdrubal Barca brought an army over the Alps — easily; the passes had been open for
eleven years and the Gauls were friendly now — and sent six riders south with a letter to his brother
naming a place to meet in Umbria.

All six were taken.

Claudius Nero, the consul facing Hannibal in the south, read the letter and did something no Roman
commander was permitted to do: he left his province. He took seven thousand picked men out of his
camp at night, leaving the fires burning and the camp at full apparent strength so that Hannibal
would not notice, and marched them four hundred kilometres north in seven days, with the towns
along the road coming out to put food and water in the soldiers' hands as they walked past without
stopping.

He joined his colleague's army in the dark, slipped his men into the existing tents so that the camp
would not look any larger from the Carthaginian side, and attacked in the morning.

Hasdrubal noticed. He saw two sets of trumpet calls in a camp that should have had one, and horses
with worn-down hooves, and he understood exactly what had happened, and he tried to withdraw in the
night and his guides deserted him and he was caught with a river at his back.

He fought it out and, when it was lost, rode into the Roman line to be killed, which the series
treats as the only decision available to a man whose family name would otherwise be on a surrender.

Nero marched his men back south in six days, arrived before he had been missed — and then had a
sack thrown over the palisade into his enemy's camp at dawn.

> *He had them bring it to him. Nobody would say what it was. He opened it himself, and looked, and
> put the cloth back over it, and said — I am told; I was not in the camp that morning — that he
> recognised in it the fate of Carthage.*
>
> *He was forty years old. He had not seen his brother in eleven years. He had come to Italy with
> three brothers and now had one, and would lose him too, at sea, within four years.*
>
> *He did not speak again that day. He moved the army south into Bruttium and held the toe of Italy
> for four more years with a force that grew smaller every season, and he was never beaten, and it
> no longer mattered in the slightest.*

## 170–172. Recalled

Masinissa was a Numidian prince who had been raised and educated in Carthage, who had commanded
Carthaginian cavalry in Spain with distinction, and who had been betrothed to Sophonisba, the
daughter of the Carthaginian commander Hasdrubal Gisco.

When his father died and his kingdom was taken by his rival Syphax, Carthage — needing Syphax more —
gave Sophonisba to Syphax instead.

Masinissa went to Rome. Everything after that follows from it.

Scipio crossed to Africa in 204 BC. He negotiated through the winter, at length and in apparent good
faith, and then, on a windless night, burned both the Carthaginian and the Numidian camps
simultaneously — wicker and reed shelters, packed close — and killed tens of thousands of men who
were asleep and unarmed and believed there was a truce.

`[A] It happened. The series films it at full length and does not let Scipio off, and has a young
tribune ask him afterwards whether it was honourable, and has Scipio give a completely honest
answer, which is that there had been no truce, only talks, and that he had been careful about the
distinction precisely so that this would be available to him.`

Syphax was defeated and captured. Masinissa rode into Cirta and married Sophonisba the same day, to
save her from being sent to Rome, and Scipio told him — kindly, and correctly under Roman law — that
she was a prisoner of the Roman people.

So Masinissa sent her poison, with a message that this was the only wedding gift he could give her,
and she drank it, and said, according to Livy, that she would have died better if she had not
married on the day of her funeral.

`[C] Livy's most novelistic scene. The outline is probably true; the speeches certainly are not. The
series keeps the outline and cuts the speeches to nine words.`

And Carthage recalled Hannibal.

He was forty-four. He had been in Italy for fifteen years. Before he sailed he went to the temple of
Juno Lacinia at Croton and had the record of his campaign — the routes, the numbers, the forces
raised, the battles — cut into bronze tablets in Punic and in Greek.

> *[The series says plainly, in narration, what this means: that sixty years later a Greek hostage
> called Polybius walked into that temple and read those tablets, and that this is the reason that
> anything true is known about Hannibal at all, and that this scene is therefore the reason the
> series can exist. Chapter 205 is the second-most important chapter in Book Five.]*

## 173–176. Zama

Two men met between the armies, with interpreters, on the day before.

The series has no idea what was said. Neither does anyone else; the speeches in Polybius and Livy
are compositions. So it does the honest thing and films it almost silently: two professionals of
enormous ability looking at each other, each knowing the other's work in complete detail, each
having spent his adult life studying the other's family.

What is said, in the series, is short. Hannibal offers Spain, Sicily, Sardinia, the islands — all
of it, everything the wars were about — in exchange for Africa and peace.

Scipio says that this was available before the burning of the camps and before Cannae and before
Saguntum, and that a man who offers to give back only what he can no longer hold is not offering
anything, and that his soldiers have not come all this way to be sent home by a treaty he could
have signed a year ago.

And then he says the true thing, which is the last line of the chapter: *If you had beaten me,
would you have taken those terms?*

Elsewhere on that field, on the flank, a Numidian rider of the tent of Iyarut — whose grandfather's
cousin died with Mathos, and whose grandfather rode in behind Naravas to save Hamilcar — looks
across a hundred paces of dust at a Carthaginian quartermaster called Safan, the last of the
Knot-keepers, who is forty-eight and has walked over the Alps and back.

They do not know each other. There is no reason they should. Their families have been entangled for
six hundred years, since an eleven-day negotiation about grazing rights, and the audience knows it
and they do not, and nothing comes of it at all.

**Zama.**

He had eighty elephants, and most of them were untrained, because the trained ones were dead in
Italy. Scipio, who had thought about this, did not form his maniples in the usual staggered
chequerboard; he formed them in columns with lanes between them, and filled the lanes with
skirmishers, and when the elephants came he had every trumpet in the army blown at once.

Some of the elephants turned and went back through the Carthaginian cavalry. The rest ran down the
lanes, hurt nobody much, and were killed at the far end.

The cavalry was the real problem, and it was a problem of arithmetic. Hannibal had spent fifteen
years winning battles with the best light horse in the world. At Zama, the best light horse in the
world was on the other side, because Masinissa was on the other side. Both Carthaginian wings were
driven off the field in the first half-hour, and pursued, and the pursuit took Masinissa and Laelius
a long way away.

Then the infantry, and it was the hardest infantry fighting of the war.

He had three lines and used them properly: mercenaries, then the African and Carthaginian levies,
then — a quarter-mile back, held out of it — the veterans of Italy. The first line fought and broke;
the second line would not let them through and they fought each other in front of the Roman advance;
the Romans came on over ground so covered with bodies and blood-slick arms that Polybius says they
had to be halted and re-formed and walked across it.

And then the last line: the men from Italy, who had crossed the Rhône with him, who had come down
the mountain with him, who had been at Trasimene and Cannae. They came forward, and Scipio's army
came forward, and the two of them stood and killed each other for a long time with no advantage on
either side at all.

That is where the battle was. And it was decided, in the end, not by either of them, but by the fact
that Masinissa's and Laelius' cavalry finished chasing the Carthaginian wings, turned round, came
back, and hit Hannibal's veterans in the back.

Twenty thousand Carthaginians died. It was, tactically, one of the best battles Hannibal ever
fought, and he lost it, because the other man had better cavalry, which was the thing Hannibal had
always had and had won every previous battle with.

> *He got away. People find that difficult. They want him to die there.*
>
> *He rode to Hadrumetum with a handful of men, and then he came to Carthage — which he had not seen
> since he was nine years old — and stood up in the Senate and told them to accept whatever terms
> were offered. A senator rose to object, and he pulled him back into his seat by the arm, and
> apologised, and said he had been in a camp since he was a child and had forgotten how men speak to
> one another in a free city.*

## 177–179. The audit

The terms: Carthage keeps Africa and nothing else. Surrenders the fleet — five hundred ships towed
out and burned in the bay where the whole city could watch. Surrenders the elephants. Pays ten
thousand talents over fifty years. And makes no war anywhere outside Africa, and none inside Africa,
without Rome's permission.

That last clause is the one that matters, and the series stops and explains it, because the audience
needs it for the last four chapters. It means that Masinissa — now king of a united Numidia, Rome's
ally, and a man who had been raised in Carthage and knew exactly what was worth taking — could annex
Carthaginian territory whenever he liked, and Carthage could not lawfully defend itself without
asking Rome for permission to do so. Which it did, for fifty years, and was refused.

And then, six years later, in 196 BC, Hannibal was elected suffete.

The last great act of his life is not a battle. It is an audit.

He went through the public accounts and found that the revenues of the state — the harbour dues, the
land tax, the tribute of the African towns — were being embezzled at scale, and that the men doing it
were the same oligarchy whose court, the Hundred and Four, held office for life and answered to
nobody. He said so publicly, with figures.

He then took to the Assembly a law making the Hundred and Four elective, annual, and ineligible for
consecutive terms — abolishing, at a stroke, the institution that had existed since the disaster at
Himera two hundred and eighty-four years earlier, and which had crucified Bomilcar, and hanged the
garrison commander of Messana, and shadowed every general in this series since chapter 35.

It passed.

And then he paid Rome's indemnity instalment, in full, on time, **without levying a single new tax**,
out of money that had previously been stolen.

> *That is the fact I would like remembered, if only one fact survives. He was a better finance
> minister than he was a general, and he was the greatest general who ever lived.*

The men he had ruined wrote to Rome to say that he was corresponding with Antiochus of Syria and
planning a new war.

`[C] Whether he was is unknowable. He probably was, at least in the sense of exploring it.`

Rome sent a commission. And Hannibal — who could count, and who had watched his own city hand men
over before — did not permit Carthage to make the decision. He attended the Senate that day as
usual, rode out of the city in the evening as if going to an estate, had a ship waiting at Thapsus,
and was gone before anyone knew.

## 180–181. A man without a city

He went first to Tyre.

Six hundred and thirty years after a woman had left that harbour with eight ships and a sack of
sand, her city's greatest son came back into it as a refugee, and was received with honours by a
provincial town in a Seleucid kingdom that had itself been conquered three times.

He stood in the temple of Melqart, to which Carthage had sent a tenth of its revenue every year for
six centuries, including — the series notes — in the years when it could not pay its own soldiers.

Then Ephesus, and Antiochus III, who wanted his name and not his advice.

Hannibal told him the only way to fight Rome was to fight it in Italy, with a fleet and a landing
and Italian allies, and to do it immediately. Antiochus preferred to fight in Greece, slowly, with a
larger army, and to keep his famous guest at court where he could be admired and not obeyed.

Asked, after a review of the royal army in all its gold and purple, whether he thought it would be
enough for the Romans, Hannibal said that he thought it would be quite enough for the Romans, greedy
as they were.

`[C] Aulus Gellius. Almost certainly polished. Used, because it is the only joke anyone recorded him
making.`

They gave him a fleet, at last, in 190 BC — a scratch Phoenician squadron off Cilicia — and the
Rhodians, who were professionals, beat him at Side.

Antiochus lost at Magnesia. The peace required the surrender of Hannibal.

So: Crete, where the story goes that he deposited jars of gold in a temple for safekeeping, filled
them with lead, and put his actual gold in hollow bronze statues in his own garden. Then, perhaps,
Armenia. Then Bithynia, and King Prusias, and a small war against the king of Pergamum, in which an
old man in his late fifties won a naval action by catapulting sealed pots full of live snakes onto
the enemy's decks.

`[C] All of the above is late, colourful and unverifiable. The series films the snakes because it is
the last victory anyone attributes to him, and it is absurd, and he is fifty-eight, and it is the
only time in Book Five that he laughs.`

## 182. Libyssa

He was living in a house at Libyssa on the Sea of Marmara, in a village of fishermen, with a small
household and seven exits that he had had cut into the walls himself, because that is the kind of
thing you do when you have spent thirty years being hunted.

Titus Quinctius Flamininus — the Roman who had liberated Greece and made a great deal of it — was in
Bithynia on other business, and raised the matter of the fugitive with Prusias, and Prusias agreed.

The series films the morning simply. A man of about sixty-four. Bad eyes — one gone since the Arno
marshes, the other failing. A servant comes in and says there are soldiers in the garden.

He asks how many, and at which exits, and the servant tells him, and he works out the answer in
about two seconds, because he has been doing that kind of arithmetic since he was nineteen.

Then he asks for the cup.

`[C] There are three traditions. Poison, kept for years in a ring or a cup. A wound in the finger,
taken while mounting a horse, that turned septic. Or Prusias having him poisoned. The dates given
are 183, 182 and 181 BC. The series uses the poison, films it, and then — in the first two pages of
chapter 183 — has a servant give all three versions to a Greek traveller, unreconciled, because
that is precisely the state of the evidence and because the audience has been trained since
chapter 21 to notice a story being made.`

What he is supposed to have said is: *let us relieve the Romans of the anxiety they have so long
endured, since it seems to try their patience too much to wait for an old man's death.*

He was about sixty-four. He had won every battle he fought on his own terms and had lost the only
war that mattered. He had not seen Carthage in twelve years, and before that had not seen it in
thirty-four, and altogether he lived in the city he destroyed himself for about nine years, all of
them before he was ten.

## 183. Liternum

In the same year, more or less, in a villa on the coast of Campania, a man of about fifty-three died
who had been prosecuted through the Roman courts for two years over money brought back from Asia,
and who, when the Senate demanded his account-books, had held them up, said that the republic could
hardly concern itself with a sum like that, and torn them in half in front of everyone.

He had saved Rome and Rome had put him on trial, which was — although nobody involved would have
enjoyed the comparison — exactly what Carthage did to its generals, and exactly what the Hundred
and Four had been created to do, and exactly what Hannibal had abolished.

He left the city and did not come back. There is a tradition that he ordered his tomb to be inscribed
*ungrateful fatherland, you shall not have my bones.*

`[C] Late. Probably invented. Kept, and immediately labelled on screen as a thing people said
afterwards.`

The two of them, the series notes without comment, died in the same season, in voluntary exile, at
the hands of the states they had saved.

## 184. Seven books

An old man in a rented room, with a table, seven finished scrolls, and a piece of filthy knotted
rope that a quartermaster gave him at Hadrumetum and asked him to keep, because there was nobody
left in that family to give it to.

He counts the knots. There are nineteen.

> *I have written what I saw and what I could check, and where I could not check it I have said so,
> which I am told is not how history is written in Rome.*
>
> *I do not expect this to survive. The men who will decide what survives are the ones who won, and
> they write extremely well, and they will not be deliberately unfair — that is the part people
> never believe. They will simply not have any reason to keep our version.*
>
> *Thirty-seven years from now, if I understand the temper of the Roman Senate, that city will be
> taken and burned and its people sold, and the libraries will be given away to Numidian kings, and
> the only Carthaginian book the Romans will trouble to translate will be a farming manual.*
>
> *So: this cord was carried out of Tyre by a girl who could splice rope, and it has nineteen knots
> in it, and the last of them was tied by a man who walked over the Alps carrying a sack of barley
> because it was the only thing he could be sure was useful.*
>
> *Nobody will ever read this. I wrote it anyway. That is also a kind of ledger.*

*End of Book Seven, and of the life of Hannibal Barca.*

*Thirty-seven years remain. He is in every one of them.*

*183–146 BC · chapters 185–200 · **Season 4**, second half*

## 185. The debt

For fifty years, Carthage paid.

Two hundred talents a year, every year, on time, out of harbour dues and land tax and the tribute of
the African towns — the revenue Hannibal had found and cleaned when he was suffete, in the one year
anybody let him govern. The men who had driven him out spent the next half-century living off the
system he built to pay the fine for the war he lost.

In 191 BC, with forty years still to run, Carthage offered to pay the entire remaining indemnity in
one payment. They had the money. They wanted it finished.

Rome refused.

`[A] Livy. Rome declined the offer. The series stages the refusal in the Senate and lets one senator
explain it to another in nine words: a debt that is paid is a leash that is dropped.`

So they went on paying, by instalments, for a war fought by men now all dead, and the city grew rich
again anyway — which was the second thing Rome held against it.

> *I saw the tablets at Croton myself, in the temple of Juno, in my forties: bronze, two columns,
> Punic on the left and Greek on the right, the routes and the numbers and the strengths of every
> force he raised, set up by the man himself before he sailed home to lose. Without them I would
> have had nothing but Roman dispatches and old men's boasting.*
>
> *I have never been able to decide whether he put them up out of pride or out of the same instinct
> that makes a drowning man reach for a rail. I have come to think there is no difference.*

## 186. The boy who was raised here

Masinissa reigned for fifty-four years and lived to ninety.

`[A] All of it: the reign, the age, the sons, the transformation of Numidia into settled grain
country, the "breadbasket" that fed Rome's armies.`

He had been a hostage-guest in Carthage as a boy, educated there, betrothed there, and had commanded
Carthaginian cavalry in Spain before the city gave his promised wife to his rival and lost him the
war it could not afford to lose. He never forgot any part of that, and he had fifty years to act
on it.

His method was patient and entirely legal. He would occupy a stretch of Carthaginian farmland —
the Great Plains, the Emporia on the Lesser Syrtis, town after town — and Carthage would appeal to
Rome, because the treaty of 201 forbade it to make war in Africa without Rome's permission. Rome
would send a commission. The commission would hear both sides at length, and then either find for
Masinissa or go home without deciding, which was the same thing.

Seventy times, according to the Carthaginians.

> *[Chapter 186 gives him one long scene, alone, very old, being read a list of estates. He asks a
> secretary whether the almond orchards above the Bagradas are on it. They are. He says that he
> climbed those trees as a boy, and that the man who taught him to ride once told him the whole
> valley was owned by four families and farmed by four thousand.*
>
> *And then, because he is the last man alive who met him: he is asked what Hannibal was like. He
> thinks about it for a long time and says: **short. Very polite. He remembered everybody's name,
> and I thought it was kindness until I realised it was inventory.**]*

In the valley itself, a family that had farmed the same fields since before there was a city on the
bay paid its rent to a Numidian king's steward instead of a Carthaginian one, and noticed very
little difference, and said so.

## 187–188. Figs, and the last instalment

Around 153 BC an old Roman senator came to Carthage on one of the arbitration commissions and saw
what everyone saw: shipping, warehouses, orchards to the horizon, a population the city could not
house, and a dockyard.

Marcus Porcius Cato was eighty. He had fought in the Second Punic War as a boy of eighteen; he had
prosecuted Scipio Africanus into retirement; he had spent his whole life arguing that Rome was being
softened by wealth and by Greeks. He came home and, according to the story, let a fresh fig drop out
of a fold of his toga onto the Senate floor and asked how far away they thought the country was that
grew it. Three days, he said. Three days' sail.

`[C] Plutarch. Too neat, and the wrong season for figs on that itinerary. The series films it as
told, and then has two senators walking out afterwards, one of whom says the fig was bought in the
Roman forum that morning, and the other of whom says that it does not matter in the slightest.`

And from then on, whatever he was speaking about — roads, grain, a rival's morals — he ended the
same way: *ceterum censeo Carthaginem esse delendam.* Besides which, I hold that Carthage must be
destroyed.

He was opposed, seriously and at length, by Publius Cornelius Scipio Nasica, who argued that Rome
should preserve Carthage **precisely because it was dangerous** — that the fear of a rival was the
only thing keeping the republic honest, and that a Rome with nothing left to be afraid of would turn
on itself within a generation.

`[A] The argument is real and the series gives it a full scene. Nasica was right, and everyone
watching in the twenty-first century knows he was right, and he lost the vote.`

In 151 BC, Carthage paid the last instalment of the indemnity. The fifty years were up. The city was
solvent, unarmed, obedient, and free of Rome for the first time since Zama.

It had eighteen months left.

## 189–192. The surrender

Masinissa took one more piece of territory, and Carthage — after fifty years, seventy arbitrations
and a final humiliation too far — sent an army against him without Rome's permission.

That was the trap and everyone in the city knew it was the trap, and the Assembly voted for it
anyway, because there is a point past which a population will not be reasonable about its own
survival. The general was a man called Hasdrubal. He was beaten; his army was surrounded on a hill
without water; he negotiated a surrender and his men were massacred as they came down.

Carthage condemned him to death in absentia, and sent word to Rome that the war party had been
punished and the offenders disowned.

Rome had already voted for war.

What followed is the strangest three months in the history of either city, and the series films it
as a slow, courteous, bureaucratic horror.

**First**, Carthage surrendered unconditionally — *deditio*, the formal placing of a city and
everything in it into the hands of the Roman people — in exchange for a promise that the
Carthaginians would keep their freedom, their laws and their territory.

**Then** Rome required three hundred children of the noble houses as hostages, and they were given.
The series films the boats. A long line of well-dressed ten-year-olds with their nurses on a quay,
and their mothers, and a Roman quartermaster checking names against a list, and the ships pulling
out past the mole into the bay.

**Then** Rome required the weapons. All of them: two hundred thousand sets of armour and two thousand
catapults, by the account we have, brought out of the arsenals in carts, for days, and stacked in
front of the consuls' camp.

`[C] Appian's numbers, and they are probably inflated. The series shows the carts and does not
count them.`

**Then**, with the city disarmed and its children in Roman hands, the consuls delivered the last
condition, which they had been holding back on instructions since the beginning:

*The Carthaginians are to leave Carthage. They may settle anywhere in their own territory they
choose, provided it is not less than ten miles from the sea. The city itself will be destroyed.*

The delegates asked them to repeat it. Then they asked where the people were supposed to live. Then
one of them asked how a nation of shipowners and carriers was expected to eat ten miles from the
water, and the consul — who was not a cruel man and had his orders in writing — agreed that it would
be difficult, and said that agriculture was a perfectly honourable way of life.

## 193. Rope

They tore the delegates apart in the street when they brought the answer back. Then they killed every
Italian in the city, which was a great many people who had done nothing. Then they freed and armed
the slaves, and elected two commanders, and recalled the man they had condemned to death, and shut
the gates.

And then a city with no weapons made weapons.

The temples became workshops. Public buildings were stripped for timber and bronze. Statues went into
the furnaces. They worked in shifts, day and night, men and women together, taking
their food by turns on a fixed roster; and Appian's reported output — **a hundred shields, three
hundred swords, a thousand catapult missiles and five hundred darts and javelins a day** — is a
figure nobody has ever been able to check and nobody has ever quite managed to dismiss.

And the torsion springs of a catapult need hair, because hair under tension is better than anything
else they had.

So the women of Carthage cut off their hair and gave it to the arsenals, and it was twisted into
cord, and the cord was wound into the springs.

`[C] Appian, *Punica* 93: for want of other fibre, the women cut off their hair for the catapult
strings. Torsion springs really were made of sinew or hair, human or horse `[A]`, so the detail is
technically sound — but it is also a siege *topos* told of other cities, and Appian is writing two
centuries later. The series stages it as reported and does not have a character comment on it.`

> *[This is the payoff of chapter 1, and the series has been holding it for a hundred and
> ninety-two chapters. The first image of the whole saga is a four-hundred-pace rope walk in Tyre and
> a girl of fourteen who splices better than her father because her hands are small. The last work
> ever done in Carthage is rope-making. The art department is instructed to reproduce the chapter 1

> courtyard, with women's hair instead of hemp.]*

> *I asked, later, in the years when I was going over the ground with the survivors' owners, what
> the mood in the city had been in those three months. I expected despair.*
>
> *What I was told, more than once, was that they were happy. That having handed over their
> children and their weapons and been told to walk inland and take up farming, the refusal was the
> first free thing any of them had done in fifty years.*
>
> *I do not think this excuses the Italians they killed in the streets. I record it because it is
> what I was told, by people who had no reason left to flatter anybody.*

## 194. Two Hasdrubals

The man they recalled from his death sentence commanded the field army outside the walls.

Hasdrubal — called the Boetharch, the relief-commander — is the last Carthaginian general in
history, and the series is precise about what he is: a brave, vain, cruel and theatrical man who has
read everything about Hannibal and has understood the least useful parts of it.

He wears purple. He keeps a bodyguard. He gives speeches. He executes people for pessimism. When the
other Hasdrubal in the city — a grandson of Masinissa, serving Carthage against his own family —
was suspected of negotiating, the Boetharch had him beaten to death in the senate house with the
benches, on the floor, in front of the government.

And late in the siege, with the city starving, he brought Roman prisoners up onto the wall in full
view of their own army and had them mutilated and killed slowly, so that his own people would
understand that there could be no surrender and no mercy for anyone who suggested one.

> *[It is Spendius' logic from chapter 107, in the same country, ninety years later, and the series
> says so by staging the two scenes identically. Carthage's last commander and Carthage's worst
> enemy solved the same problem the same way.]*

In the same month, the best cavalry officer left in Carthaginian service — a man called Hamilcar,
known as the Samnite — rode out with his squadron and went over to the Romans.

## 195–197. The siege

It should have been quick, and for three years it was not.

The first consuls were incompetent and nearly lost their army twice. The city was enormous, walled
across the isthmus in three layers with chambers in the wall for three hundred elephants and four
thousand horses `[C]`, and it held.

What the war produced instead was a young Roman officer who kept rescuing other people's mistakes:
Publius Cornelius Scipio Aemilianus, aged about thirty-five, a military tribune when the siege began.

His ancestry is not a coincidence the series invents; it is simply true, and it is the reason this
man is standing here.

- His natural father was Lucius Aemilius Paullus, the conqueror of Macedon.
- His natural grandfather was the Lucius Aemilius Paullus **who was killed at Cannae**.
- He was adopted into the family of Scipio Africanus, and was that man's grandson by adoption.

`[A] All documented.`

The grandson of the consul Hannibal destroyed at Cannae, and the heir of the man who beat Hannibal
at Zama, was in the same body, outside the walls of Carthage.

In 147 BC the Assembly at Rome elected him consul although he was standing for a different office
and was below the legal age — the law was set aside by popular vote, exactly as it had been set
aside for his adoptive grandfather thirty-eight years earlier when nobody else would take Spain.

He closed the isthmus with a wall of his own, so that nothing came in by land. Then he built a mole
across the mouth of the harbour, stone by stone, in the water, to close the sea as well. It took
months, and the Carthaginians watched it grow, and made no attempt to stop it, which the Romans
found encouraging.

They had been digging a new channel, by hand, in secret, from the inner harbour out to the open sea
on the other side; and they had been building ships out of the roof timbers of their own houses.

One morning the water opened where there had been no water, and fifty triremes came out of a city
that had surrendered every weapon it owned three years before.

> *[The series treats chapter 197 as its last moment of joy, and gives it to the losing side. It is
> the most extraordinary engineering feat of the entire war, and it accomplishes nothing at all:
> they win a small action, hesitate, retire, and are bottled up again within days. That is the
> chapter's argument and the series' argument. Brilliance is not a strategy.]*

That winter, Masinissa died at ninety. Scipio, whom the old king had asked for by name, arrived too
late and divided the kingdom between his three sons — which is how Rome inherited Numidia too.

## 198–199. Six days

By the spring of 146 BC the city was eating whatever was in it.

Scipio took the harbour quarter, and then the market, and then began to move up the three narrow
streets that climbed from the forum to the citadel on the Byrsa hill.

Carthage was built six storeys high, with alleys wide enough for two men — the same streets in which
the citizens had fought Bomilcar's coup a hundred and sixty years before, and won.

It took six days.

The legions went up the streets and were fought from the windows, the doorways and the roofs. So
they went onto the roofs, laying planks from one building to the next, and fought along them, and
the men fighting on the planks fell into the streets, and the men in the streets were fighting over
the bodies. Scipio's engineers came behind and set fire to the blocks that had been taken and pulled
them down to clear a road for the machines, and the demolition teams did not have time to check the
houses, so what came down into the street came down with people in it — the old, the hidden, the
ones who had not been able to get out — and the road was levelled over them so the carts could pass.

Six days and six nights, with the troops relieved in shifts because men could not keep it up longer
than that.

On the seventh day the Byrsa asked for terms, and fifty thousand people came out — perhaps a tenth
of what the city had held — and were sold.

## 200. The wife of Hasdrubal, and the ledger

Nine hundred Roman deserters held out in the temple of Eshmun at the top of the hill, because they
had nothing to be given.

Hasdrubal the Boetharch went out to Scipio alone, without telling them, and asked for his life, and
was granted it, and knelt.

His wife came out onto the roof of the burning temple with their two sons, where the whole army
could see her, and where he could.

`[A]/[C] Appian, following Polybius, who was there. The speech he gives her is certainly composed.
The series cuts it to four lines.`

She called down to Scipio that he was a Roman and doing a Roman's work and she held nothing against
him. Then she spoke to her husband, and called him a coward and a traitor and the man who had sold
the city he had killed people for, and asked what he intended to tell his sons.

Then she cut their throats, threw them into the fire, and went in after them.

And the deserters set light to what was left of the roof and went into it too.

> *[The series has been waiting a hundred and eighty-two chapters for this. In chapter 18, a woman
> who would not let a city be inherited built a pyre in the open where the men who had demanded her
> could see it, and stepped into it, and bound them by her death. In chapter 200, on the same hill,
> a woman does it again — and this time it binds nobody, saves nothing, and is watched by a hundred
> thousand people who will never speak Punic again.*
>
> *Same staging. Same lens. Same fire. Six hundred and seventy-nine years apart. Do not underline
> it in dialogue. Let the composition do it.]*

Carthage burned for seventeen days.

> *I stood next to him while it burned. He had been my pupil; he was forty; he had just done the
> largest thing any Roman had ever done.*
>
> *He was crying, and he was not hiding it, and after a long time he took hold of my hand and said
> that he was afraid. Not of the gods, and not of the Carthaginians, of whom there were none left.
> He said that he had a feeling of terror, that a day would come when the same order would be given
> about his own city, and he quoted Homer, the lines about the day when sacred Troy shall perish —*
>
> *— and I asked him, because I was his teacher and it was my right, what he meant.*
>
> *And he said, plainly and by name, that he meant Rome.*

`[A] Polybius reports this himself, and it is one of the few passages in ancient literature where a
historian records a private conversation to which he was the only witness and asks us to believe him.
The series believes him.`

The Senate sent a commission of ten. The harbour was demolished, the site cursed, and a law passed
forbidding anyone to live there. The territory became the province of Africa, and the estates of the
Carthaginian nobility were sold to Roman investors, and the tenants stayed on the land and paid the
new owners.

The libraries were given away to the kings of Numidia.

Out of six hundred and eighty years of writing — the treaties, the laws, the accounts, the voyages,
the histories, whatever Carthage had said about itself for thirty generations — the Roman Senate
ordered exactly one work to be translated into Latin and preserved:

**Mago's twenty-eight books on farming.**

> *So that is the ledger, and I am the one who is permitted to write it.*
>
> *A woman left Tyre with eight ships and a sack of sand, and bought a hill from people who did not
> think a hill could be bought, and paid them rent for two hundred years, and then stopped.*
>
> *The city she left behind grew rich enough to buy armies, and clever enough to fear the men who
> commanded them, and it put them on trial one after another for three hundred years, and the last

>
> *He is the only one of them anybody will remember, and he will be remembered in the language of
> the people who killed him, out of my books, which are in Greek, and which I wrote in the house of
> the family that destroyed his.*
>
> *There is a farm above the Bagradas that has been worked by one family since before the ships
> came. They paid a Punic landlord, and then a Numidian king, and now they pay a Roman syndicate.

>
> *They are the only thing in this entire account that was not destroyed.*

- **Hamilcar I at Himera.** Herodotus' self-immolation, filmed through smoke so it stays ambiguous.
- **The tophet.** Rare, elite, crisis-driven; disputed on screen by Carthaginians; Diodorus'
  500 children presented explicitly as Diodorus' claim.
- **Hamilcar Barca's death.** Both traditions in one image — drowning, with a burning ox-cart behind.
- **The Alpine pass.** Col de la Traversette, never named on screen.
- **Ticinus.** The son saves the father, with a Ligurian slave silently in frame.
- **Cannae's command.** Responsibility divided between Varro and Paullus rather than following
  Livy's scapegoating of Varro.
- **Maharbal's line.** Used, then refuted at length.
- **Hannibal's death.** Poison at Libyssa, c. 183 BC — with the two rival versions and all three
  dates reported in the following chapter, unreconciled.
- **Cato's fig.** Staged as told, then undercut on screen by two senators who know where it was
  actually bought.
- **Appian's numbers** for the surrendered weapons and the daily arms output during the siege:
  shown, never counted aloud, never confirmed.
- **The speech of Hasdrubal's wife.** Cut from Appian's composed oration to four lines.

## Invented — never presented as history

- **The Knot-keepers** (house of Yatonbaal) and the knotted cord: Elishat, Bititt, Bodo, Adonibaal,
  Milkat, Bostar, Safan.
- **The tent of Iyarut**: Tikat, Ayzid, Zamar, Massiv, Iyarut the younger.
- **The Rutilii of Reate**: Manius and Caeso Rutilius.
- All private dialogue, all interior life, all domestic scenes, and the specific personal motives
  assigned to documented actions.
- The interiority of Sosylos. **The man is real** `[A]` — Hannibal's Greek tutor, who accompanied him
  and wrote seven books that are lost. Everything he thinks and feels in this series is invented;
  the fact that he wrote and that the books vanished is not.

## Deliberately excluded

- **Aeneas and Dido.** Eleven centuries out of place and invented by Virgil to explain the Punic
  Wars backwards. Not available to this story at any price.
- **Anything supernatural.** No verified omens, no prophecy, no fate.
- **The salting of the fields.** A nineteenth-century invention with no ancient source whatsoever.
  Chapter 200 shows the demolition, the curse on the site and the law forbidding resettlement — all
  documented — and never mentions salt.
- **Any suggestion that Carthage could have been saved after 149 BC.** Rome had voted for the war
  before the first embassy sailed. Book Eight is a tragedy, not a thriller, and contains no
  near-misses.

# Story Bible — CARTHAGE SAGA

**Series title:** カルタゴ・サガ / **CARTHAGE SAGA**
**Shape:** 4 seasons · 8 Books · 200 chapters · 100 episodes · c. 825–146 BC · English text
**Status:** `PROPOSED` in full. Nothing here is `CANON` until the author approves it.
**Factual floor:** `story/historical-dossier.md`. **Narrative text:** `story/THE-STORY.md`.
**Arc and chapter map:** `story/arcs.md`.

---

## 1. Logline

`PROPOSED` — Over six centuries, a city founded by refugees becomes the richest power in the
western sea by learning to buy everything — land, soldiers, gods, peace — until it produces one
man who tries to pay Rome back in the only currency it never learned to counterfeit, and discovers
that winning every battle is not the same as winning.

**Hannibal's own line** (from Book Five onward): *A general who is never defeated in the field, and
whose life is one long defeat.*

**And the series' line, which only lands in the last sixteen chapters:** *he loses, and then it takes
Rome fifty-six more years to finish what he made necessary — and the people who pay for it are the
same people who were paying rent in chapter 8.*

## 2. Premise

Carthage begins as a boat full of frightened people with stolen money. It ends, in 146 BC, as ash
and a slave market, and the series goes all the way there. Between those two points it invents something
genuinely new: a state that makes war with other people's bodies and its own silver, that answers
to merchants rather than kings, and that puts its victorious generals on trial. The saga follows
that invention across twenty-odd generations, through four viewpoint families, to the man it was
all building toward.

Hannibal Barca is the protagonist and the emotional centre. Everything before him exists so that
when he stands at the top of the Alps and points at Italy, the audience understands **exactly** what
is behind him, what it costs, who pays, and why it will not be enough. Everything after him — the
sixteen chapters of Book Eight — exists so that the audience sees what his defeat actually bought,
and who it was finally charged to. He dies in chapter 182 and he is the subject of every chapter
after it.

## 3. Genre & tone

`PROPOSED` — **Seinen historical drama.** Closest comparisons: *Vinland Saga* (tonal register,
violence with consequence, philosophical spine, long silences), *Kingdom* (scale of warfare, but
slower and less triumphalist), *Vagabond* (interiority, stillness between violence).

Register: grounded, unromantic, dry-mouthed. Dialogue is plain and adult. No power system, no
magic, no chosen ones. Gods are real to the characters and silent to the camera. Battles are
logistical before they are heroic. Reader age 16+.

The show is not "Carthage good, Rome bad." It is two functioning societies that each contain
decent people, and neither can stop.

## 4. Themes

**The question the story argues about: *what is a civilisation worth, and who is sent to pay for it?***

Threaded arguments, each carried by characters rather than narration:

| Theme | Carried by |
|---|---|
| **Debt.** Everything in Carthage is a debt — to Tyre, to the Libyans, to the mercenaries, to Rome, to the dead. The story is a ledger that never balances. | The Knot-keepers; the indemnity; Hannibal's audit as suffete |
| **The oath and the choice.** Is Hannibal fulfilling a nine-year-old's vow, or using it as cover for a policy he chose as an adult? The series never resolves this — it asks it in every arc. | Hannibal, Hamilcar, Sosylos |
| **Empire from below.** Carthage began as a tenant on Libyan land and ended as its landlord. Every Carthaginian triumph is somebody's harvest. | The tent of Iyarut; Mathos; Masinissa |
| **Who owns the soldier.** A state that hires its armies will one day find its armies deciding who the state is. | The Truceless War; the Barcid army in Iberia |
| **The uses of the past.** Every generation tells the founding story differently to justify what it currently wants. Elissa is a martyr, then a fool, then a goddess, then a warning — and in ch. 200 a woman does the real thing again and it saves nothing. | The five "founding story retold" scenes |
| **Winning is not the same as not-losing.** Rome's genius is that it can lose indefinitely. Carthage's tragedy is that it can only win. | Fabius; the sixteen Italian years |
| **What survives.** One narrator writes for a future that burns his books; the other survives because he lived in the winners' house, and says so. | The Sosylos → Polybius handover, ch. 185 |
| **The long bill.** A defeat is not an event, it is a payment schedule. Zama is not the end of Carthage; it is the instrument that makes the end legal, and it takes 56 more years. | Book Eight entire |

## 5. Protagonist

**HANNIBAL BARCA** `[A]` — see `characters/CHR-01-hannibal.md`. Named in ch. 87, first seen aged
nine in ch. 115, protagonist from ch. 134, dead in ch. 182 — and the subject of every one of the
sixteen chapters that follow his death.

He is not introduced as a genius. He is introduced as a nine-year-old who wants his father's
attention and gets a religious oath instead. His competence is built on screen: logistics, languages,
patience, and a frightening capacity to predict what frightened men will do. His flaw is the same
as his gift — he understands armies perfectly and states not at all. He can destroy any Roman army
and cannot make Rome surrender, because surrender is a political act and politics is the one field
where he is an amateur.

**Book-level protagonists before him** (each fully realised, each handed off):

| Book | Chapters | Protagonist | `Tier` |
|---|---|---|---|
| One | 1–22 | **Elissa of Tyre** — the founder | `[C]` legend, treated as a person |
| Two | 23–60 | **Hanno the Navigator**, then **Bomilcar** | `[A]`/`[B]` |
| Three | 61–92 | **Hamilcar Barca** as a young officer; **Regulus** as his Roman mirror | `[A]` |
| Four | 93–117 | **Hamilcar Barca**; **Mathos** the Libyan as co-protagonist | `[A]` |
| Five | 118–136 | **Hannibal**, becoming himself; **Hasdrubal the Fair** as the road not taken | `[A]` |
| Six | 137–157 | **Hannibal** | `[A]` |
| Seven | 158–184 | **Hannibal**, with **Scipio** as the second protagonist from ch. 163 | `[A]` |
| Eight | 185–200 | **Scipio Aemilianus** and **Hasdrubal the Boetharch** — and Hannibal, absent, in all of it | `[A]` |

Continuity viewpoint characters run under all of them — see §10.

## 6. Antagonist / opposing force

There is no villain. There are four opposing forces, and the story rotates which one is in front:

1. **Rome** `[A]` — not evil; *unstoppable in a specific way*. Rome can absorb Cannae. Rome's
   institutions survive its defeats; Carthage's institutions survive its victories badly. Rome's
   compelling faces: **Regulus** (Book Three), **Fabius Maximus** (Book Six), **Marcellus**,
   **Claudius Nero**, **Scipio Africanus**, **Scipio Nasica** and **Scipio Aemilianus** (Book
   Eight), and **Cato**. Each is given a scene where he is right — Nasica's is the argument that
   Rome should spare Carthage because a republic with nothing to fear will turn on itself, and he
   loses the vote.
2. **Carthage's own constitution** `[A]` — the Hundred and Four, the trials of generals, the
   auctions of office. The state that cannot let a man win without suspecting him of wanting a crown.
3. **The subject peoples** `[A]`/`[B]` — the Libyans and Numidians who have every reason to want
   Carthage broken, and who in the end are the ones who break it.
4. **Time and money** — the indemnity, the interest, the silver running out, the sons dying before
   the fathers. The only antagonist that never negotiates.

**Hanno II "the Great"** `[A]` is the nearest thing to a recurring personal antagonist, and the
bible's standing instruction is: **he is right more often than the audience wants him to be.** He
believes Carthage is a trading state that cannot survive a land empire. He is wrong about Hamilcar
and correct about the outcome.

## 7. Core conflict

A mercantile republic that wins by buying, against a military republic that wins by refusing to
lose — and, inside Carthage, a family of soldiers against the merchants who pay them.

## 8. Story engine

Repeatable trouble, generation after generation:

> **Carthage needs something it cannot make — silver, grain, soldiers, safety — so it reaches
> outward. Reaching outward means either paying someone or fighting someone. Paying creates a
> creditor; fighting creates a general. Carthage fears both. It solves the fear by destroying
> whichever man succeeded. Then it needs something again.**

Every Book is one turn of that wheel, at a larger scale and a higher price. The Barcids are the
generation that refuses the last step — and Book Five is what happens when the wheel finally breaks.

## 9. Rules the story must not break

1. **No invented history presented as history.** Invented *people* may stand beside real ones.
   Invented *events* fit only into gaps the sources leave. No invented battle, treaty, city, king
   or campaign, ever.
2. **Outcomes are fixed.** Himera is lost. Regulus lands. The Aegates are lost. Cannae is won.
   Zama is lost. Hannibal dies in exile. No alternate history, no near-misses invented for suspense.
3. **No side is clean.** Any episode that makes Rome monstrous must be paired, inside two chapters,
   with Carthage doing something comparable — and the reverse.
4. **No prophecy, no destiny, no supernatural confirmation.** Omens occur because people believe
   in them; the camera never verifies one.
5. **Violence has aftermath.** No battle chapter is followed immediately by another battle chapter.
   The chapter after a battle is always about what the battle cost somebody who did not choose it.
6. **Hannibal is never shown to be magic.** Every one of his victories is set up beforehand —
   ground, weather, hunger, the enemy commander's temperament — so the audience can see it coming
   and the Romans cannot.
7. **The audience always knows what the money is doing.** Silver is a character.
8. **No anachronistic nationalism.** Nobody dies for "Carthage" as a nation-state; they die for
   families, gods, pay, cities, and men they know. Hannibal's army has no common language;
   the story never forgets it.
9. **Languages are real and shown.** Punic, Libyan, Greek, Latin, Iberian, Gaulish. Interpreters
   are characters. Mistranslation is a plot device at least three times.
10. **Elephants are small.** North African forest elephants, roughly 2.5 m at the shoulder,
    terrified of noise, dangerous to their own side. `[A]`

## 10. Continuity viewpoint families — the four ropes

Four invented `[F]` lineages give the six-century span a human floor. Each appears in every Book;
each has exactly one member alive as a viewpoint character at a time; none of them changes a
historical outcome. They are the "ordinary people" register the series lives on.

| Line | Who | Function | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The Knot-keepers** — house of Yatonbaal | Tyrian rope-makers who sail with Elissa; later harbour workers, shipwrights, rowers, quartermasters of Carthage | Carthage seen from the docks. Keep a **knotted family cord**, one knot per generation — the series' central object | `[F]` |
| **The tent of Iyarut** | Libyan farmers of the Bagradas valley, later Numidian riders | Carthage seen by the people it taxes. From landlords of the first city to rebels in the Truceless War to Masinissa's cavalry at Zama | `[F]` |
| **The Rutilii of Reate** | Sabine smallholders; a rower at the Aegates, a legionary at Cannae | Rome seen from the ranks — so the audience cannot hate Rome wholesale | `[F]` |
| **The house of Sosylos** | *Sosylos of Sparta* is **real** `[A]` — Hannibal's Greek tutor, who accompanied him and wrote seven lost books on his deeds. The series makes him its narrator. | The frame. He is writing the story the audience is watching, and his books do not survive | `[A]` person, `[F]` interiority |

**The knotted cord.** A length of ship's rope, one knot tied per generation, carried from Tyre in
825 BC. By Hannibal's day it is an arm's length of filthy cord that no one outside one family can
read. It appears in every Book. In the final chapter it is given to Sosylos to keep with his
manuscript — and the audience has been told, from episode one, that Sosylos' books were lost.

## 11. Season and Book structure

**4 seasons · 8 Books · 200 chapters · 100 episodes.** Each season is exactly two Books.
Full breakdown in `story/arcs.md`.

| Season | Book | Chapters | Eps | Period | Role |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **One — The New City** | 1–22 | 10 | c. 825–800 BC | Foundation. The debt, the rope, the Libyan landlords, Elissa's death and the invention of her legend |
| | **Two — The Merchant Sea** | 23–60 | 16 | c. 750–264 BC | Trade, Mago, Alalia, the first treaty with Rome, **Himera and the birth of the Hundred and Four**, Hanno's Atlantic voyage, the Crimissus, **Agathocles in Africa**, Bomilcar's coup, Pyrrhus |
| **2** | **Three — The Sea Between** | 61–92 | 14 | 264–241 BC | The First Punic War from the rowing benches and from Eryx. Ends with Hamilcar ordered to sign his own surrender |
| | **Four — Truceless** | 93–117 | 11 | 241–237 BC | The Mercenary War; the Libyan reckoning; Sardinia stolen; **the oath at the altar** |
| **3** | **Five — Silver** | 118–136 | 9 | 237–219 BC | Iberia; Hamilcar's death; Hasdrubal the Fair and the Ebro treaty; Hannibal takes command; Saguntum |
| | **Six — The Road** | 137–157 | 15 | 218–216 BC | Alps; Trebia; Trasimene; Fabius; **Cannae**; and the argument about what a victory is for |
| **4** | **Seven — The Long Defeat** | 158–184 | 15 | 216–183 BC | The sixteen years; Scipio in Spain; the Metaurus; **Zama**; the suffeteship and the audit; exile; Libyssa |
| | **Eight — The Ashes** | 185–200 | 10 | 183–146 BC | The indemnity generation; Masinissa; Cato and Nasica; the surrender; **the refusal**; the three-year siege; the six days; the fall |

**Pacing.** Books One and Two compress centuries and are built as linked novellas, each with its own
complete protagonist arc. Books Three onward run in continuous time. The compression ratio tightens
all the way to Cannae — roughly 60 years per chapter at the start, **eight hours across four
chapters** at ch. 153–156 — then opens out again for Book Eight, which covers 37 years in 16
chapters on purpose: after Hannibal, time starts moving quickly again, and that is the point.

**Where Hannibal is.** Born in ch. 87 (a line in a dispatch). First seen in ch. 115. Protagonist
from ch. 134. Dies in ch. 182. Present, as cause and as absence, in all sixteen chapters after that.

## 12. Ending

**Known and fixed. Four beats, each quieter than the audience expects:**

- **Ch. 182 — Libyssa.** An old man in a rented house on the Sea of Marmara asks how many soldiers
  are in the garden and at which exits, works it out in two seconds, and asks for the cup. `[C]` The
  rival versions and all three dates are reported afterwards by a servant, unreconciled.
- **Ch. 184 — Seven Books.** Sosylos finishes, with the knotted cord on the table. *Nobody will ever
  read this. I wrote it anyway. That is also a kind of ledger.* **This is not the end of the series.**
  It is the end of the man who loved him, and the audience is allowed to think it is the end.
- **Ch. 185 — the handover.** Sosylos' scrolls are sold for the blank backs of the sheets, and
  **Polybius** takes the narration: *Mine will survive. I want that understood at the start… because
  I was in the right house.*
- **Ch. 200 — The Wife of Hasdrubal, and the Ledger.** The burning temple; the woman on the roof
  doing what Elissa did on the same hill 679 years earlier, and binding nobody; the deserters going
  into the fire; Scipio Aemilianus — grandson of the consul killed at Cannae, adoptive grandson of
  the man who beat Hannibal — weeping and quoting Homer and naming Rome. Then the libraries given
  away, the one book Rome keeps, and the family above the Bagradas who have outlived three empires
  and whose name nobody ever wrote down.

**The final image is not Hannibal, and it is not the fire.** It is a field, and the people still
working it, and a narrator admitting whose house he wrote in.

## 13. What the audience must feel, Book by Book

| Book | The feeling |
|---|---|
| One | *These people are refugees and they are not going to make it.* Then they do. |
| Two | *This is the richest place in the world and something is badly wrong with it.* |
| Three | *Rome is not going to stop. Nobody in Carthage understands that yet.* |
| Four | *The bill for two hundred years has arrived, and it is being paid by the wrong people.* |
| Five | *Oh. This is what all of that was for.* |
| Six | *He is going to win everything, and you are going to watch him find out that it is not enough.* |
| Seven | *Nobody is coming. Not for him, not for anyone.* |
| Eight | *This did not have to happen — and it was always going to.* |

## 14. Decisions taken

The author's instruction: **make historically grounded choices that best serve a dramatic
anime/manga adaptation; prioritise plausibility while allowing strong character, drama, action and
emotion.** The six open questions are therefore closed as follows. All remain `PROPOSED` and
reversible; none is `CANON` yet.

**1. Length and shape — `RESOLVED`.**
**200 chapters, 8 Books, 4 seasons, 100 episodes.** The earlier 212-chapter draft was cut to 200 by
compressing Books One–Two (the pre-Punic centuries) from 96 chapters to 60, and 16 new chapters were
added for the fall of Carthage. Pre-Hannibal history is now 30% of the runtime rather than 45%.
Chapters 1–22 were not renumbered, so existing chapter-1 production work stands. Concordance in
`arcs.md`.
*Why:* four seasons is the stated target, and the compression falls entirely on the material the
audience is least attached to — while the sections that carry the drama (Truceless War, the oath,
the Alps, Cannae, Zama, the fall) all kept or gained room.

**2. Elissa — `RESOLVED`: the reconstruction, with the legend built on screen.**
Book One dramatises a real dynastic exile buying land on an annual rent; the ox-hide and the
Iarbas-romance are shown being *composed* in ch. 21 by a priest, twenty years later, in front of the
woman who was actually there. `[C]` throughout.
*Why:* the legend is demonstrably a Greek invention (the *byrsa* pun) and Virgil's Aeneas is eleven
centuries out of place. Staging the invention rather than the myth costs nothing dramatically, gives
Book One a thesis, and sets up the series' running argument about who writes things down — which is
what ch. 185 and ch. 200 are finally about.

**3. Tophet — `RESOLVED`: rare, elite, crisis-driven, and contested on screen.**
Shown once, in ch. 56, during the Agathocles panic — in fragments, from outside a wall, with two
Carthaginians arguing bitterly about whether it is happening at the scale claimed, and Diodorus'
"five hundred children" framed explicitly as a Greek writer's assertion. Never shown again. Never
used to characterise Carthage as a whole. **The practice is neither denied nor confirmed by the
camera.**
*Why:* the scholarship is genuinely unresolved (Xella et al. 2013 against Schwartz et al. 2010), the
hostile tradition is obviously motivated, and this is the single most defamed thing about a real
civilisation with living descendants. Treating it as a disputed atrocity rather than a national
character trait is both the more honest reading and the more interesting scene.

**4. Sosylos — `RESOLVED`: voice-over only until ch. 124.**
One wordless appearance in ch. 1 (hands, desk, scrolls; face never in frame), paid off in ch. 184.
He is a working historian embedded with his subject, says so, and flags his own uncertainty out
loud — *I was not there; I had this from a man who; treat it as you please.*
**Extended by decision 5:** Polybius takes the narration in ch. 185 and appears on screen from
ch. 196. He is real, he was there, he recorded the Homer scene himself, and he is honest about being
a client of the family that did it.
*Why:* it gives the series a legitimate way to say "the sources disagree" without breaking the drama,
and it turns the evidence problem into a character relationship.

**5. The 146 BC ending — `RESOLVED`: dramatised in full, as Book Eight.**
Sixteen chapters, ten episodes, the second half of Season 4.
*Why:* the author's call, and it repairs the one structural weakness of the earlier draft. Hannibal's
tragedy is legible as a personal one when the series stops at Libyssa; it is legible as a
*historical* one only when the audience watches the clause he signed at Zama — no war in Africa
without Rome's leave — get used, patiently, legally, for fifty-five years, by a man he once
commanded. **Book Eight is not an epilogue. It is the verdict.** Hannibal remains the emotional
centre because every chapter of it is downstream of him: the revenue system he built pays the
indemnity, the treaty he signed licenses the strangulation, the general who commands the last defence
is a man performing a bad imitation of him, and the Roman who burns the city descends from both the
consul he destroyed at Cannae and the man who beat him.

**6. Language — `RESOLVED`: multilingual, with a hard convention.**
- **Punic** — the series' "home" language. Rendered in plain English, standard balloons.
- **Every other language** — Libyan, Greek, Latin, Iberian, Gaulish, Numidian — rendered in English
  in a **distinct typeface and balloon shape**, one per language, fixed for 200 chapters.
- **When the POV character does not understand, the audience does not either.** The words stay
  untranslated on the page and unsubtitled on screen. This happens in ch. 8 (the rent negotiation),
  ch. 96 (Autaritus translating with emphasis), ch. 141 (the Alpine guides) and ch. 173 (the parley).
- **Interpreters are named characters with their own agendas**, and mistranslation is a plot device
  three times.
- Anime: real-language performance for short exchanges; the home language in Japanese (or the dub's
  language) with the same untranslated-when-uncomprehended rule.
*Why:* Hannibal's army had no common tongue and that fact is load-bearing — it is why Autaritus
matters, why the hostage system works, and why an army that never lost a battle could never become
a nation. Rendering it costs a font in manga and a few lines of ADR in the anime.

## 15. Still open

Genuinely undecided, and needing the author:

1. ~~**CHR-34's name**~~ — **`RESOLVED`, 2026-09-20: he is Yatonbaal.** The house of Yatonbaal is
   named for the man who ties the first knot; the name then recurs down the line by Phoenician
   papponymy, so the Knot-keepers alternate two names for six centuries and the audience meets a
   Yatonbaal again centuries later. **The second name in the alternation is still open**, and is
   needed only when ch. 22 (Elishat's son ties the second knot) is written.
2. **Whether Book Eight's narrator change is announced or discovered.** Current draft announces it
   in Polybius' first lines. The alternative is to let the audience notice, three chapters in, that
   the voice is no longer the one they have listened to for 184 chapters.
3. **Season 1's commercial risk.** No Hannibal for 26 episodes. Mitigations are in place (the anime
   cold open, five self-contained protagonists, the ch. 60 finale) but a co-production partner may
   still push for a 13-episode Season 1 covering ch. 1–60 at 4.6 chapters per episode. That cut
   exists and it is worse.

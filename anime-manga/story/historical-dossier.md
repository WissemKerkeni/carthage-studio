# Historical Dossier — Carthage, 1200–146 BC

**Status: RESEARCH BASE. `PROPOSED` as the factual floor of the series.**
This file is the *history*, not the story. Nothing invented for drama belongs here except
where explicitly tagged `[F]`. `story/THE-STORY.md` may dramatise anything in here; it may
not contradict an `[A]` line without a `RETCON` entry.

## Evidence tier legend

| Tag | Meaning | How the story may use it |
|---|---|---|
| `[A]` | **Documented.** Attested in surviving ancient sources and/or archaeology; broadly accepted by modern scholarship. | Outcome is fixed. Dialogue and interior life are free. |
| `[B]` | **Likely interpretation.** A reconstruction most scholars would accept, or the majority reading of thin evidence. | May be shown as fact on screen; flagged in the appendix. |
| `[C]` | **Disputed / uncertain.** Sources conflict, numbers are implausible, or scholars actively disagree. | Pick one reading, stage it, and say in the appendix that it is a choice. |
| `[F]` | **Fiction.** Invented for this series. | Must never be presented to the audience as recorded history. |

A hard rule for this project: **no invented battle, treaty, siege, king or campaign is ever
presented as history.** Invented *people* may stand beside real ones; invented *events* must
fit inside the gaps the sources leave.

## The source problem — and why it is a story problem

`[A]` Carthage was destroyed in 146 BC and its archives with it. Almost every written word we
have about Carthage was written by Greeks and Romans — that is, by the people who fought it.
`[A]` The nearest thing to surviving Carthaginian literature is the *Periplus* of Hanno, and
that only in a Greek translation. `[A]` Mago's agricultural treatise survived only because the
Roman Senate ordered it translated after the city burned; the rest of the libraries were given
away to Numidian kings and lost.

`[B]` Our two main narrators of the Punic Wars are Polybius — a Greek hostage in Rome, patron-bound
to the family of Scipio, writing some sixty years after Cannae, and the best source we have — and
Livy, writing under Augustus: dramatic, patriotic, and demonstrably unreliable on numbers.

**Series consequence.** The saga is told by people who know their words will be burned. That is
not a framing gimmick; it is the historical condition of the subject. See `story/lore.md`.

# PART ONE — PHOENICIAN ORIGINS (c. 1200–800 BC)

| Tier | Date | Fact |
|---|---|---|
| `[A]` | c. 1200–1100 BC | Bronze Age collapse. Egyptian and Hittite power recedes from the Levant; the Canaanite coastal cities — Byblos, Sidon, Arwad, **Tyre** — survive and fill the vacuum by sea. |
| `[A]` | c. 969–936 BC | **Hiram I** of Tyre: timber and craftsmen to Solomon, joint Red Sea ventures, the temple of Melqart rebuilt. Tyre becomes the richest Phoenician city. |
| `[A]` | c. 887–856 BC | **Ithobaal I** expands Tyrian territory and styles himself "King of the Sidonians." |
| `[A]` | from 858 BC | **Assyrian tribute.** Shalmaneser III, then Tiglath-Pileser III (744–738), Sargon II, Sennacherib, Esarhaddon, Ashurbanipal. Sidon is destroyed by Esarhaddon and its people enslaved. Tyre survives by paying, and by being an island. |
| `[B]` | 9th–8th c. BC | **Westward colonisation is partly flight.** Tribute, deportation and war push Tyrian capital and people west. Utica, Gadir (Cádiz) and Lixus are traditionally dated to the 12th–11th c. BC `[C]`; archaeology supports the 9th–8th. |
| `[A]` | 585–573 BC | **Nebuchadnezzar II besieges Tyre for thirteen years.** The city is not destroyed but is broken as an independent power. |
| `[A]` | 332 BC | **Alexander storms Tyre** after seven months, building a mole out to the island. Some 2,000 are crucified, the rest enslaved. Phoenician independence ends. Carthage is by then the heir, not the branch. |

**Why Phoenicians went west** `[B]`: silver (Iberia, Sardinia), tin (the Atlantic route), copper
(Cyprus), murex for purple dye, and grain. Phoenician colonisation was a *chain of anchorages*,
one day's sail apart — not a land empire. Carthage inherited that logic and then broke it.

# PART TWO — THE FOUNDATION (c. 825–800 BC)

## The legend `[C]`

Told by Timaeus (4th c. BC) and by Pompeius Trogus via Justin:

- Elissa (Greek: **Dido**) and her brother **Pygmalion** are joint heirs to the throne of Tyre; the people take Pygmalion alone.
- She marries her uncle **Acerbas / Sicharbas / Zakarbaal**, priest of Melqart — the second man in the city.
- Pygmalion murders him for his hidden gold.
- Elissa flees with a faction of nobles, tricking her brother's men by throwing sacks of sand overboard as her dead husband's treasure, then implicating the crew in the "loss" so they dare not return.
- Cyprus: a priest of Astarte joins her on condition that his line hold the priesthood for ever; **eighty women** are taken from the shore to be wives for her men.
- Africa: the local ruler grants her as much land "as an ox-hide will cover"; she cuts the hide into a single thread and rings the hill — **Byrsa**.
- **Iarbas** of the Maxitani demands her in marriage or war; she builds a pyre, says she goes to her husband as they wished, and kills herself.

`[B]` **What is defensible.** Pygmalion (Pu'mayyaton) is a real Tyrian king — the **Nora Stone**
from Sardinia (9th c. BC) is read by many as naming him. Menander of Ephesus' king-list, preserved
by Josephus, includes both Pygmalion and Elissa and yields a flight c. 825 BC. A dynastic split
throwing off a colonial expedition is entirely ordinary for the period.

`[C]` **What is invention.** The ox-hide trick turns on a Greek pun (*byrsa* = hide); the Punic
name was probably *Bosra*, "citadel." Michael Grant argued Elissa was originally a goddess
rationalised into a queen. **Aeneas never met her** — Virgil wrote that romance around 19 BC,
eleven centuries out, to give Rome's wars with Carthage a mythic cause.

## The date `[C]`

| Reading | Basis |
|---|---|
| **814 BC** | Timaeus' traditional date, transmitted by Roman writers. Still the conventional one. |
| **c. 825 BC** | Menander/Josephus king-list arithmetic. |
| **c. 760–740 BC** | The earliest securely dated archaeological layers — Levantine pottery, the first structures on the Byrsa. |

`[B]` **Most likely:** a real late-9th-century foundation whose earliest, smallest phase is hard to
see archaeologically, with the traditional 814 approximately right in date and wholly legendary in
detail. **Series choice:** the story opens c. 825 BC and never states a year on screen.

## The first city `[B]`

- Carthage = **Qart-ḥadašt**, "New City" — new relative to **Utica**, which was already old and never let Carthage forget it.
- `[A]` It paid **rent to the Libyans** for its land, reportedly until about the mid-6th century BC. The great power of the west began as a tenant.
- `[A]` A tithe was owed to the temple of Melqart at Tyre, and was still being sent centuries later.

# PART THREE — GROWTH, SEA POWER, AND THE MAGONIDS (c. 750–480 BC)

| Tier | Date | Fact |
|---|---|---|
| `[A]` | 8th–7th c. | Carthage grows on the carrying trade: metals west, manufactures east. Exports include terracottas, masks, jewellery, carved ivories, ostrich-egg vessels, wine, salt fish and garum. |
| `[A]` | c. 650 BC | Carthaginian settlement on **Ibiza**; the western network begins to be *Carthaginian* rather than Tyrian. |
| `[A]` | 6th c. | **Mago I** founds the Magonid house (dominant c. 550–340 BC). Carthage becomes pre-eminent among the western Phoenician cities. |
| `[C]` | 540–535 BC | **Battle of Alalia** (Sardinian Sea). Sixty Phocaean Greek ships against 120 Carthaginian and Etruscan. The Greeks hold the field but lose two-thirds of their fleet — a "Cadmean victory" — and abandon Corsica. Etruria takes Corsica; **Carthage takes Sardinia**. |
| `[A]` | 509 BC | **First treaty with Rome** (Polybius). Rome may not sail west past the Fair Promontory; shipwrecked Romans get five days and repairs only; Carthage will not fortify in Latium or harm Rome's coastal dependents. The sea is divided — and Rome is the junior party. |
| `[B]` | c. 525–510 BC | Magonid campaigns in Sardinia (Hasdrubal I and Hamilcar I); Hasdrubal reportedly dies of wounds there. Sardinia becomes Carthage's grain and silver estate. |
| `[A]` | 480 BC | **Battle of Himera.** **Hamilcar I** invades Sicily; Gelon of Syracuse destroys the army. Hamilcar dies — killed, or, in Herodotus' version, walking into his own sacrificial fire when he sees the battle lost `[C]`. |
| `[A]` | after 480 BC | Defeat breaks the Magonid monarchy's grip. Power passes to the **Council of Elders** and the courts. Carthage turns inland and south — Africa and the Atlantic — for some seventy years. |

## Exploration `[A]` / `[C]`

- `[A]` **Hanno the Navigator** (5th c. BC): the *Periplus*, 101 lines surviving in Greek translation. A colonising expedition down the Atlantic coast of Africa — sixty ships and a reported 30,000 people `[C]` (the number is widely doubted). Seven settlements founded in what is now Morocco. Landmarks: the volcanic "Chariot of the Gods," fires and drums along the night shore, and finally the hairy people the interpreters call **Gorillai**, three of whose skins were said to hang in a temple at Carthage until 146 BC.
- `[C]` How far: Senegal at minimum; Sierra Leone, Cameroon or Gabon argued. The text may be deliberately vague — trade routes were state secrets.
- `[C]` **Himilco** sailed north in the same era, toward Brittany and perhaps Britain, seeking tin. His account survives only in fragments quoted centuries later, full of weed-choked seas and windless dark — plausibly disinformation.

# PART FOUR — THE STATE AND THE SOCIETY

## Constitution `[A]` / `[B]`

`[A]` **Aristotle** (*Politics* II.11) praises Carthage as a *mixed* constitution — monarchic,
aristocratic and democratic elements together — and says it had never, to his knowledge, suffered
a serious revolution or a tyrant. He faults it for one thing: **offices are effectively for sale**,
and men are chosen for wealth as much as for merit.

| Organ | Tier | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Suffetes** (*šōfeṭīm*, "judges") | `[A]` | Two, elected annually. Civil and judicial heads of state. By the 3rd c. BC they do **not** command armies. |
| **Adirim** ("the Mighty Ones"), the Senate | `[A]` | Council of Elders, several hundred strong; law and foreign policy. |
| **Council of 104** | `[A]` | Tribunal of judges, holding office for life. Aristotle calls it the highest constitutional authority; it exists chiefly to **judge generals**. |
| The Thirty / inner committee | `[B]` | A small standing board within the Senate that did the real business. |
| **Popular Assembly** | `[A]` | Voted when suffetes and Senate disagreed, and (with the army) on commanders. Grew in weight over time. |

`[A]` **Generals were appointed separately from magistrates, and judged afterwards.** A defeated
Carthaginian commander could expect prosecution; `[B]` crucifixion of failed generals is reported
often enough that the threat was real, even if every individual case comes from a hostile source.
**This is the central political engine of the series:** a state that sent men to war and then put
them on trial for how the war went.

## Religion `[A]` / `[C]`

- `[A]` Chief deities **Baal Hammon** and **Tanit** (*Tinnit pene Baal*, "Tanit, face of Baal"), who
  becomes pre-eminent; **Melqart** of Tyre, the Barcid family god, whom Greeks equated with Heracles;
  **Eshmun** the healer, whose temple crowned the Byrsa; **Astarte**; and later **Demeter and Kore**,
  adopted in 396 BC `[A]` after a Carthaginian army sacked their shrine at Syracuse and then died of plague.
- `[A]` The **tophet**: a walled precinct of urns holding cremated infant remains, often with lamb or
  kid bones, under stelae carrying the sign of Tanit and votive formulas. Carthage's alone has yielded
  **more than 20,000 urns** and 6,000 stelae.
- `[C]` **Child sacrifice — the live debate.**
  - *For*: Stager and Greene, and Xella et al. (2013) — the age profile (overwhelmingly under three
    months), the careful deposition, the standardised vow formula on the stelae, and a consistent
    hostile Greek and Roman tradition.
  - *Against*: Tunisian and Italian scholars from the mid-20th century, and Schwartz et al. (2010) —
    a normal infant-mortality profile and evidence of perinatal death, i.e. a cemetery for children
    too young for ordinary burial, libelled by their enemies.
  - **Series choice** `[B]`: stage it as *rare, elite and crisis-driven* — the thing the city does
    when it is terrified; argued about by Carthaginians themselves on screen; and endlessly
    exaggerated by Greeks and Romans who want a monster. `[A]` Diodorus' account of 500 children
    given in the panic of 310 BC is dramatised **as Diodorus' account**, not as fact, with a
    Carthaginian character disputing it in the scene.

## Army and navy `[A]` / `[B]`

- `[A]` Citizen infantry is small and mostly a home-defence and elite force; the field armies are
  Libyan subjects, African and Iberian levies, and hired professionals.
- `[A]` **The Sacred Band** — some 2,500 citizens of good family, best-equipped, fighting as a
  phalanx. Prominent at the **Crimissus (339 BC)**, where it was destroyed.
- `[A]` **Libyan** heavy infantry — the disciplined core. **Numidian** light cavalry — javelins, no
  saddle or bridle, the best skirmishing horse in the Mediterranean. **Iberian** *scutarii* and
  *caetrati* with the falcata. **Balearic slingers** (from c. 480 BC). **Gauls** — long swords, mass,
  and no patience.
- `[A]` **War elephants** from the 3rd century: the now-extinct North African forest elephant, smaller
  than the Indian. `[C]` Whether they carried towers is disputed; the animal was probably too small
  for most of the towers later art gives it.
- `[A]` The **navy** is where citizens serve — rowing and fighting was paid, stable, lower-class
  employment and a pillar of political stability. `[A]` The **cothon**: a rectangular merchant
  harbour opening into a circular naval basin with an island command post and ship-sheds for a
  reported 220 hulls. `[A]` The **Marsala ship**, a 3rd-century Punic warship wreck, carries
  **assembly letters** — Carthage built warships from numbered prefabricated parts.

## Economy and land `[A]` / `[B]`

- `[A]` **Mago's** twenty-eight books on agriculture were famous enough that the Roman Senate had
  them translated after 146 BC. Carthage practised intensive, irrigated, scientific estate farming
  in Cape Bon and the Bagradas valley — the **chora**.
- `[B]` **The Libyan question.** The African subject population paid heavy tribute — reportedly up to
  **half** the harvest in wartime `[C]` — and supplied soldiers. They revolted whenever Carthage was
  weak: with Agathocles, in the Mercenary War, and finally with Rome. A great deal of Carthaginian
  wealth is rent extracted from Africans by a city that began as their tenant.

# PART FIVE — SICILY: THE THREE-HUNDRED-YEAR WAR (480–265 BC)

`[A]` Sicily is the axis on which everything turns: seven named wars between Carthage and the Greek
cities, essentially one war with long truces.

| Tier | Date | Event |
|---|---|---|
| `[A]` | 480 | **Himera** — Gelon of Syracuse destroys Hamilcar I's army. Carthage pays 2,000 talents. |
| `[A]` | 410–404 | **Second war.** **Hannibal Mago** — grandson of the Hamilcar killed at Himera, and avowedly avenging him `[B]` — sacks **Selinus** and **Himera**; 3,000 prisoners are reportedly killed on the spot where his grandfather died `[C]`. |
| `[A]` | 406 | **Akragas** falls after eight months. Hannibal Mago dies of plague in the siege lines; **Himilco II** takes over and sacks Gela and Camarina. |
| `[A]` | 398–393 | **Dionysius I of Syracuse** storms **Motya** — and introduces the catapult to the world. Himilco sacks Messana, reaches Syracuse, and is annihilated by **plague**. He returns home, accepts the blame publicly, and starves himself to death `[C]`. |
| `[A]` | 383–376 | **Cabala** and **Cronium**. Carthage wins; Dionysius pays 1,000 talents. |
| `[A]` | 345–339 | **Timoleon** and the **Crimissus** — a Carthaginian army caught crossing a flooded river in a hailstorm. The **Sacred Band** is destroyed. |
| `[A]` | 311–306 | **Agathocles.** Beaten at the Himera river and besieged in Syracuse, he does the unthinkable: sails to **Africa (310 BC)**, lands near Cape Bon and **burns his own ships**. For four years a Greek army camps within sight of Carthage's walls. |
| `[A]` | 310–308 | Carthage's crisis. Libyan subjects defect. Agathocles murders his Libyan ally **Ailymas**, then invites **Ophellas of Cyrene** to join him and murders him too, absorbing his army. |
| `[C]` | 310 | Diodorus: in the terror, 500 children of noble families are sacrificed — 200 given up, 300 volunteered — because the nobles had been substituting bought children for their own. *The single most lurid passage about Carthage in any ancient source; treat as Diodorus' claim.* |
| `[B]` | 308 | **Bomilcar's coup.** A Carthaginian general attempts to seize the city with his own troops while the Greeks are at the gates; the citizens fight him in the streets and he is executed — reportedly crucified in the marketplace, still denouncing them. |
| `[A]` | 307–306 | Agathocles' African army collapses. **He abandons it and escapes to Sicily**, leaving his own sons behind to be killed by his men. Peace on the old line: Carthage west of the Halycus. |
| `[A]` | 279/278 | **Treaty with Rome against Pyrrhus** — mutual aid, Carthaginian transports, each state paying its own men. The last time they are allies. |
| `[A]` | 278–276 | **Pyrrhus of Epirus** in Sicily takes Eryx and Panormus, fails at **Lilybaeum**, and leaves saying — in Plutarch's report `[C]` — "What a wrestling-ground we are leaving, my friends, for the Romans and the Carthaginians." |

**Series reading** `[B]`: Carthage never wanted to *rule* Sicily; it wanted the western ports and no
strong single power on the island. That defensive, mercantile aim is why it fought there for two and
a half centuries without ever finishing the job — and why Rome, which finishes jobs, broke it.

# PART SIX — THE FIRST PUNIC WAR (264–241 BC)

| Tier | Date | Event |
|---|---|---|
| `[A]` | 288 | The **Mamertines**, Campanian mercenaries, seize **Messana** and live by raiding. |
| `[A]` | 265–264 | Pressed by Hiero II of Syracuse, they appeal to *both* Carthage and Rome. A Carthaginian garrison arrives first. Rome's Senate hesitates; the **Assembly votes for war**, expecting profit. The garrison commander lets himself be talked out of the city — and is crucified for it `[C]`. |
| `[A]` | 263 | Hiero II of Syracuse changes sides and stays Rome's ally until his death. |
| `[A]` | 262 | **Agrigentum.** Roman siege; Hanno's relief force beaten. The city is sacked and **25,000 inhabitants sold into slavery** — this is what tells the Sicilian Greeks what Rome is. |
| `[A]` | 260 | Rome builds a fleet. **Mylae** — the **corvus** boarding-bridge turns a sea fight into an infantry fight; some fifty ships taken. |
| `[A]` | 256 | **Cape Ecnomus** — around 680 warships and perhaps 290,000 men engaged; possibly the largest naval battle in history `[C]` (Polybius' numbers, debated). Roman victory opens Africa. |
| `[A]` | 256–255 | **Regulus** lands, wins at Adys, takes Tunis. Carthage sues for peace; his terms are so harsh the Senate refuses them. |
| `[A]` | 255 | **Tunis.** The Spartan mercenary **Xanthippus** reorganises the Carthaginian army — elephants in front, cavalry on the wings, phalanx in line — and destroys Regulus, who is captured. `[C]` The Roman legend of Regulus paroled to Rome, arguing against his own exchange, returning to be tortured to death, is late, patriotic and probably invented. |
| `[A]` | 255 | The evacuation fleet is wrecked in a storm: **384 of 464 ships lost**; perhaps 100,000 men lost to weather in a single season `[C]`. |
| `[A]` | 251 | **Panormus** — Metellus baits the elephants onto stakes and javelins and captures ten; they are paraded in Rome. |
| `[A]` | 249 | **Drepana** — **Adherbal** wins Carthage's greatest naval victory. Consul Claudius Pulcher had thrown the sacred chickens overboard first: "if they will not eat, let them drink" `[C]`. **Phintias** follows; Rome is swept from the sea for seven years. |
| `[A]` | 247–241 | **Hamilcar Barca** takes command in Sicily. From **Heircte** and then **Mount Eryx** he wages a mobile, amphibious, raiding war and is never beaten in the field — with an army Carthage barely pays. |
| `[A]` | 241 | **Aegates Islands.** Rome builds a last fleet on private loans; Catulus catches the Carthaginian grain convoy heavy and undermanned and destroys it. The Eryx army is cut off. **Hamilcar is ordered to negotiate his own defeat.** |
| `[A]` | 241 | **Treaty of Lutatius:** evacuate Sicily, return prisoners, pay **3,200 talents over ten years**. Sicily becomes Rome's first province. |
| `[A]` | 237 | **Sardinia.** While Carthage is prostrate, Rome declares its attempt to retake its own rebel island an act of war, and takes **Sardinia and Corsica** plus **1,200 talents** more. |

`[A]` **Polybius' judgement**, and the hinge of the whole saga: the seizure of Sardinia was "the
single greatest cause" of the next war. Rome won the First Punic War and then stole the peace.

# PART SEVEN — THE TRUCELESS WAR (241–238 BC)

`[A]` Polybius calls it the most savage war he knows of.

- `[A]` Some 20,000 unpaid soldiers of the Sicily army are shipped home in batches, concentrated at **Sicca**, and haggled with by a state that has run out of money and credibility. Their demands rise as the haggling drags on.
- `[A]` Leaders: **Spendius**, a runaway Roman slave who knows he will be crucified if there is a settlement; **Mathos**, a Libyan who knows what Carthage does to Libyans; **Autaritus**, a Gaul who speaks Punic and therefore controls what the polyglot camp hears.
- `[A]` The Libyan countryside rises — 70,000 join `[C]`. **Utica** and **Hippacritae**, Phoenician cities centuries old, massacre their Carthaginian garrisons and go over. Utica offers itself to **Rome**, which — remarkably — refuses.
- `[A]` **Hanno the Great** commands and bungles it (Utica, 240). **Hamilcar Barca** is given a second army by popular demand and wins on the **Bagradas** by a feigned retreat that turns into a reversal of front.
- `[A]` **Naravas**, a Numidian chief, rides into Hamilcar's camp alone to change sides, and is promised Hamilcar's daughter. His cavalry decides the war.
- `[A]` Hamilcar offers captured rebels enlistment or freedom. To make surrender impossible, **Spendius and Autaritus torture 700 prisoners to death**, **Gisco** among them — hands cut off, legs broken, buried alive. After that Hamilcar tramples prisoners under elephants. Neither side takes prisoners again; hence *truceless*.
- `[A]` **The Saw** (238): Hamilcar pens some 40,000 rebels in a defile until they eat their horses, then their slaves, then surrender and are destroyed. Spendius is crucified. **Mathos** is taken at Leptis Parva and torn apart by the crowd in the streets of Carthage.
- `[A]` Outcome: Carthage survives; the chora is devastated; the Barcid house is now the only institution with a functioning army; and Rome has taken Sardinia. `[B]` The war is *also* Carthage's colonial reckoning. The Libyans were not wrong.

# PART EIGHT — THE BARCIDS IN IBERIA (237–219 BC)

| Tier | Date | Event |
|---|---|---|
| `[A]` | 237 | **Hamilcar Barca** crosses to Iberia — marching the army along the coast to the Straits `[B]`, because Carthage cannot spare a fleet. Officially: to find money for the indemnity. |
| `[A]` | 237 | **The oath.** Polybius has it from Hannibal himself, told decades later to Antiochus III: aged nine, begging to come, he is taken to the altar and made to swear **never to be a friend of Rome**. `[C]` Whether the adult Hannibal meant a personal vendetta or was justifying a policy is exactly the question this series is about. |
| `[A]` | 237–228 | Hamilcar builds a *state*: silver mines, tribal treaties, hostages, **Akra Leuke**, and an army paid from Iberian silver and loyal to him personally. `[B]` He sends money home, which buys the Assembly, which keeps Hanno the Great off his back. |
| `[C]` | 228 | **Hamilcar dies** covering his sons' retreat across a river — drowned, or killed by the Oretani using burning ox-carts. Accounts differ. |
| `[A]` | 228–221 | **Hasdrubal the Fair**, his son-in-law, succeeds — elected by the army, confirmed at home. He rules by marriage, diplomacy and money rather than war; the Iberians reportedly acclaim him as their supreme commander. He founds **Qart-ḥadašt / New Carthage (Cartagena)**: the best harbour in the western Mediterranean, with silver mines, arsenals and a palace. |
| `[A]` | c. 226 | **The Ebro treaty** — Hasdrubal agrees with Roman envoys that Carthaginian arms will not cross the **Ebro**. `[C]` Was it ratified at Carthage? Did it bind Hannibal? Did it contain a clause about Saguntum? Polybius and Livy disagree; the ambiguity was probably deliberate on both sides. |
| `[A]` | 221 | Hasdrubal is **assassinated** by a Celt whose master he had executed; the man dies under torture, smiling `[C]`. |
| `[A]` | 221 | The army acclaims **Hannibal**, aged 26, and Carthage confirms him. **Hanno the Great speaks against it** and is voted down. |
| `[A]` | 221–220 | Campaigns against the **Olcades** (Althia), the **Vaccaei** (Helmantice, Arbucala), and the great victory at the **Tagus** over a Carpetani-led coalition — the first battle that is recognisably *his*: refuse the crossing, let them come into the water, kill them in it. |
| `[A]` | 219 | **Saguntum** — a city south of the Ebro that has made itself a Roman friend. Hannibal besieges it for **eight months** and takes it. He sends the plunder to Carthage; it buys the Senate `[B]`. |
| `[A]` | 218 | Roman embassy at Carthage. **Quintus Fabius Maximus** gathers a fold of his toga and says he carries peace and war in it; take which you please. The Senate answers: **you choose**. He lets fall war, and they shout that they accept it. |

# PART NINE — THE SECOND PUNIC WAR (218–201 BC)

## The march

- `[A]` Leaves **New Carthage** in late spring 218. Garrisons Iberia under his brother **Hasdrubal**; sends Iberian troops to Africa and African troops to Iberia — hostage-exchange as policy.
- `[A]` Polybius: he enters Gaul with some **50,000 foot and 9,000 horse** (figures vary); crosses the **Rhône** in September, floating **thirty-seven elephants** over on earthed-over rafts; and evades Scipio's army by marching upriver.
- `[A]` **The Alps**, about fifteen days, October–November: Allobroges ambushes in the gorges, a rockfall broken by fire and — in Livy — vinegar `[C]`, snow on old snow on the descent, pack animals going over the edge. He arrives with **20,000 foot and 6,000 horse**, barely half, and few elephants.
- `[C]` **The pass.** Disputed since antiquity. **Col de la Traversette** now has the best physical case: Mahaney's churned peat layer with horse-gut bacteria, radiocarbon-dated to about 218 BC. Clapier and Montgenèvre remain argued. **Series choice:** Traversette, unnamed on screen.

## The victories

| Tier | Date | Battle |
|---|---|---|
| `[A]` | Nov 218 | **Ticinus** — a cavalry action. Consul **P. Cornelius Scipio** is wounded and saved — by his seventeen-year-old son `[B]`, or by a Ligurian slave `[C]`. |
| `[A]` | Dec 218 | **Trebia** — Numidians bait the Romans across an icy river before breakfast; **Mago** rises out of the reed beds behind them. Most of the elephants die that winter. |
| `[A]` | Jun 217 | **Trasimene** — a four-day march through the flooded Arno marshes, in which **Hannibal loses an eye** to infection; then the lake road in fog and the destruction of **Flaminius'** army. Some 15,000 dead, 10,000 taken: the worst ambush in Roman history to that date. |
| `[A]` | 217 | **Fabius Maximus**, dictator: shadow him, never fight him, burn the country. Hannibal escapes the Falernian passes by driving **cattle with torches tied to their horns** up a ridge at night. Rome names Fabius *Cunctator*, the Delayer — at first as an insult. |
| `[A]` | Aug 216 | **Cannae.** Some 86,000 Romans under **Varro** and **Paullus** `[C]`; a crescent of Gauls and Iberians giving ground on purpose; Libyan veterans in captured Roman armour on the flanks; **Hasdrubal's** heavy horse destroying one wing, then riding the length of the field to take the other from behind. **At least 67,500 killed or captured** `[C]`, including Paullus, two ex-consuls, twenty-nine of forty-eight tribunes, and some eighty senators. |
| `[C]` | 216 | **Maharbal:** "You know how to win a victory, Hannibal; you do not know how to use one." Livy's line, quite possibly invented — and the series' central argument about whether Rome could have been taken at all. |

## The long defeat

- `[A]` Capua, Tarentum, much of the south and the Greek cities defect; the **Latin colonies and central Italy do not**. That is the war.
- `[A]` Rome's answer: never fight him, fight everyone else. Twenty-five and more legions in the field; slaves and debtors enlisted; a whole generation under arms for sixteen years.
- `[A]` **215**: Philip V of Macedon allies with Hannibal — Rome buys the Aetolians and fights that war with other people's soldiers.
- `[A]` **213–211**: **Syracuse** falls to Marcellus despite Archimedes' engines; Archimedes is killed. **Capua** falls in 211. Hannibal marches on Rome to break the siege — *Hannibal ad portas* — and it does not work; the land his camp stands on is auctioned in the Forum during the siege, at full price `[C]`.
- `[A]` **211**: **Upper Baetis** — the Scipio brothers are defeated in detail and killed after their Celtiberian hirelings are bought off.
- `[A]` **210**: **Publius Cornelius Scipio**, twenty-five, holds no qualifying office and is given Spain by popular vote because no one else will take it.
- `[A]` **209**: **New Carthage** stormed in a day, wading a lagoon at low water. Scipio takes the treasury, the arsenals and the **Iberian hostages** — and sends them home free: the mirror of Hannibal's policy, and better politics.
- `[A]` **208**: **Baecula** — Scipio wins; Hasdrubal extricates his army and marches for Italy. **Marcellus** is killed in an ambush.
- `[A]` **207**: **The Metaurus.** Hasdrubal's dispatch to his brother is intercepted. **Claudius Nero** marches 7,000 men some 400 km north in seven days, secretly, joins his colleague, and destroys Hasdrubal, who rides into the ranks to die. `[A]` **His head is thrown into Hannibal's camp.** Livy: Hannibal said he recognised the doom of Carthage.
- `[A]` **206**: **Ilipa** — Scipio inverts his own battle order at dawn and attacks before the Carthaginians have eaten, ending Carthaginian Iberia. A mutiny at **Sucro** over pay follows; Scipio crushes it.
- `[A]` **205–203**: **Mago** lands in Liguria, achieves nothing decisive, is wounded at Insubria and dies at sea. **Masinissa** — raised at Carthage, once betrothed to **Sophonisba**, once Carthage's cavalry commander in Spain — goes over to Rome after his father's death and the loss of his kingdom to **Syphax**, who has married Sophonisba instead.
- `[A]` **204–203**: Scipio lands in Africa. He negotiates through the winter — then burns both the Carthaginian and Numidian camps by night, killing tens of thousands. **Great Plains**; Syphax captured; **Sophonisba** takes poison sent by Masinissa rather than walk in a Roman triumph `[C]` — the most novelistic scene in Livy; the outline is probably true, the speeches are not.
- `[A]` **203**: Hannibal is recalled after fifteen years. He leaves an inscription of his campaign in Punic and Greek at the temple of Juno Lacinia at Croton — **Polybius later reads it**, which is why the numbers we have are as good as they are.

## Zama and after

- `[A]` **202, Zama** (site unlocated `[C]`): the parley between the two men; **eighty elephants** stampeded through deliberately opened lanes; two lines of veterans that will not break each other; and then **Masinissa's and Laelius' cavalry returning from the pursuit** into Hannibal's rear. Some 20,000 Carthaginian dead.
- `[A]` **201, terms:** keep Africa, lose everything else; surrender the fleet and the elephants; **10,000 talents over fifty years**; make no war outside Africa, and none inside it without Rome's leave. `[B]` That last clause is the knife: it hands Masinissa a licence to eat Carthage by inches, legally.
- `[A]` **196, Hannibal as suffete.** He audits the state, exposes the embezzlement of public revenue, makes the **Hundred and Four** elective and annual instead of lifelong, and pays Rome's indemnity **without new taxes**. The men he ruins write to Rome.
- `[A]` **195**: Rome demands him. He does not let the city choose; he rides out to a prepared ship and is gone. **Tyre** first — the mother city, after some six hundred years — then **Ephesus** and **Antiochus III**.
- `[A]` **190**: given a fleet at last, and beaten by the Rhodians at **Side**. **Magnesia**: Antiochus loses; the peace requires Hannibal's surrender.
- `[A]` / `[C]` Flight: Crete; then Armenia and Artaxata `[C]`; then **Prusias I of Bithynia**; and the sea-fight won by catapulting jars of live snakes onto Eumenes' decks `[C]`.
- `[C]` **c. 183–181 BC, Libyssa.** Flamininus demands him; the house is ringed with soldiers; he takes poison. Or he dies of a wound. Or Prusias poisons him. Atticus and Livy say 183; **Polybius says 182**; another source 181. `[C]` The parting line — *let us relieve the Romans of the anxiety of waiting for an old man's death* — is too good to be true and too good to omit.
- `[A]` Scipio Africanus dies in retirement at Liternum in about the same year, having been hounded through the courts by **Cato**. `[C]` "Ungrateful fatherland, you shall not have my bones" is later tradition.
- `[A]` **149–146 BC:** Masinissa's encroachments, Carthage's illegal self-defence, and Cato's *ceterum censeo*. Scipio Aemilianus takes the city after six days of street fighting; the survivors are sold. `[A]` Polybius, standing beside him, reports that he wept and quoted Homer on the fall of Troy — knowing Rome's turn would come one day.

# PART TEN — THE LONG PEACE AND THE THIRD PUNIC WAR (201–146 BC)

*Added when the series was extended to the fall of Carthage. This part is the factual floor for
**Book Eight** (chapters 185–200).*

## The fifty years

| Tier | Date | Fact |
|---|---|---|
| `[A]` | 201–151 BC | Carthage pays the 10,000-talent indemnity in fifty annual instalments — and **pays it off in full, in 151 BC**, having recovered economically to the point of embarrassment. |
| `[B]` | 191 BC | Livy (36.4) reports that Carthage offered to pay the **entire remaining indemnity in a single payment**, and that Rome declined and took only the year's instalment. *Verify against Livy before scripting; the series treats it as the thesis scene of ch. 185 — a debt that is paid is a leash that is dropped.* |
| `[A]` | 201–148 BC | **Masinissa** repeatedly seizes Carthaginian territory. Carthage, forbidden by the treaty of 201 to make war in Africa without Rome's leave, petitions Rome instead. **Rome backs Masinissa or declines to decide.** |
| `[A]` | 152 BC | **Cato the Elder**, a veteran of the Second Punic War, serves on one of these arbitration commissions and is alarmed by Carthage's wealth. |
| `[C]` | c. 152–149 BC | The **fresh figs** produced in the Senate to show how near Africa lies. Plutarch; polished, and the season does not quite work. |
| `[A]` | 152–149 BC | *Ceterum censeo Carthaginem esse delendam* — appended by Cato to every speech he makes, on any subject. |
| `[A]` | 152–149 BC | **P. Cornelius Scipio Nasica Corculum** — son-in-law of Scipio Africanus and the most influential man in the Senate — argues **against** destroying Carthage: that the fear of a rival is what keeps Rome united and restrains the people. *He is the most important dissenting Roman voice in the series, and he loses.* |
| `[A]` | 150 BC | **Oroscopa.** Carthage finally campaigns against Masinissa without Roman permission — a treaty breach — and the army is surrounded on waterless ground, surrenders, and is largely massacred. **Hasdrubal** escapes to Carthage and is **condemned to death in absentia** to placate Rome. |
| `[A]` | 149 BC | Rome declares war. It had already decided to; the embassies that follow are procedure, not negotiation. **Utica defects to Rome first** — as it had in 240 BC. |

## The surrender, in four steps `[A]`

This sequence is documented and is the spine of chapters 190–192. It should be staged as
bureaucracy, not melodrama.

1. **Deditio.** Carthage surrenders itself unconditionally to the Roman people, in exchange for a
   promise of freedom, laws and territory.
2. **Hostages.** Three hundred children of the noble families, handed over and shipped to Sicily.
3. **Disarmament.** Every weapon in the city, carried to Utica in convoys. Surviving records give
   **200,000 sets of armour and 2,000 catapults** `[C]` — almost certainly inflated, and never
   counted aloud on screen. The warships sail to Utica and are burned.
4. **The last condition**, held back on instructions until the city is defenceless: **abandon
   Carthage and settle at least ten Roman miles (16 km) from the sea.** The city itself to be
   destroyed.

## The refusal and the re-armament

- `[A]` The envoys bringing the answer are killed by the crowd; the Italians in the city are
  massacred; the slaves are freed and armed; the gates are shut.
- `[A]` Appian: temples and every unoccupied space become workshops; men and women work day and
  night in shifts, taking food by roster.
- `[C]` **Daily output, per Appian:** 100 shields, 300 swords, 1,000 catapult missiles, 500 darts
  and javelins, and as many catapults as they could build. Uncheckable; universally repeated.
- `[C]` **The women cut off their hair for the catapult strings**, for want of other fibre.
  Technically sound — torsion springs were made of sinew or hair, human or horse `[A]` — but it is
  also a siege *topos* told of other cities, and Appian writes two centuries later.
  **This is the single most important image in the series (ch. 193), and it is a `[C]`.** Stage it
  as reported; let no character comment on it.

## The siege, 149–146 BC

| Tier | Date | Fact |
|---|---|---|
| `[A]` | 149–147 | Two years of Roman failure. The city's triple landward wall across the isthmus holds. `[C]` Its chambers are said to have housed 300 elephants and 4,000 horses. |
| `[A]` | 149–146 | **Hasdrubal the Boetharch** commands the field army. He beats a colleague — a grandson of Masinissa serving Carthage — to death in the senate house on suspicion of treating with Rome, and later has Roman prisoners mutilated on the walls in full view of their own army. |
| `[B]`/`[C]` | 147 | **Hamilcar the Samnite**, a Carthaginian cavalry commander, deserts to Rome. Appian; not in the standard summaries. Treat as `[C]` and keep — it is one line and it costs nothing if wrong. |
| `[A]` | 148 | **Masinissa dies, aged about ninety**, after a reign of some fifty-four years. Scipio Aemilianus divides the kingdom between his three sons. |
| `[A]` | 147 | **Scipio Aemilianus** — standing for the aedileship — is elected **consul**; public demand is such that the Senate suspends the age requirements for every office that year. *The exact mechanism that gave his adoptive grandfather Spain in 210 BC.* |
| `[A]` | 147 | Scipio seals the isthmus, then builds a **mole across the harbour mouth**. The Carthaginians **cut a new channel from the harbour to the open sea**, having secretly built a fleet, and sail out. The sortie achieves nothing decisive. |
| `[A]` | spring 146 | **Six days** of street fighting from the harbour up to the Byrsa, through six-storey housing and alleys two men wide; planks laid roof to roof; taken blocks fired and demolished, over the people still inside, to clear a road for the siege machines. |
| `[A]` | 146 | On the last day Scipio accepts prisoners — **except the 900 Roman deserters**, who hold the temple of Eshmun and burn it over themselves. |
| `[A]` | 146 | **50,000 Carthaginians surrender and are sold into slavery.** This figure is consistent across the sources. |
| `[A]`/`[C]` | 146 | **Hasdrubal surrenders alone** and is granted his life. **His wife**, on the temple rampart, blesses Scipio, curses her husband, and goes into the fire with their two children. `[C]` Whether she killed them first (Appian) or walked in with them is a variant; the speech Appian gives her is composed. She is **unnamed in every source**. |
| `[A]` | 146 | Polybius, standing beside Scipio, reports that Scipio **wept**, took his hand, quoted Homer on the day sacred Troy shall perish, and — asked what he meant — named **Rome**. *Polybius is the only other witness and asks us to believe him.* |
| `[A]` | after 146 | The site is demolished and cursed; resettlement is forbidden by law; the territory becomes the province of Africa. **The libraries are given away to the kings of Numidia**, and the Senate has exactly one Carthaginian work translated into Latin: **Mago's twenty-eight books on agriculture.** |
| `[F]` | — | **The salting of the fields never happened.** There is no ancient source for it whatsoever; it is a nineteenth-century invention. The series never mentions salt. |

## Disputed numbers in Book Eight

- Appian's **84,000** Roman soldiers at the landing: modern estimates are **40,000–50,000**.
- Carthage's population: estimates range from **90,000 to 800,000**. The series never states one.
- The **200,000 panoplies / 2,000 catapults**: shown, never counted.
- The daily arms production and the hair: `[C]`, as above.

## Why Book Eight belongs to this series

`[B]` The treaty Hannibal signed at Zama in 201 forbade Carthage to make war in Africa without
Rome's permission. For fifty-two years, Masinissa — who had been raised in Carthage, betrothed to a
Carthaginian, and had commanded Carthaginian cavalry before Carthage gave his promised wife to his
rival — used that clause to take Carthaginian land at leisure, and Rome adjudicated in his favour.
The war of 149 began the moment Carthage finally defended itself.

**The instrument of Carthage's destruction is the peace treaty its greatest general was made to
sign.** That is not a thematic reading imposed on the evidence; it is the mechanism the sources
describe. It is the reason this series does not end at Libyssa.

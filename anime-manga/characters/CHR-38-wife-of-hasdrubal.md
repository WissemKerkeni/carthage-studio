# CHR-38 — THE WIFE OF HASDRUBAL

## Identity
- **ID:** CHR-38 · **Name:** **unnamed in the sources** `[A]`, and **unnamed in this series** — a
  standing decision, not an oversight
- **Role:** the final scene of the saga · **Appears:** ch. 193, 198, 200 · **Status:** `PROPOSED`

> **Evidence note.** She is real `[A]`/`[C]`: Appian, following Polybius, who was present, describes
> the wife of Hasdrubal appearing on the roof of the burning temple of Eshmun with their two sons,
> denouncing her husband to Scipio and to his face, killing the children and throwing herself into
> the fire. The event is attested; **the speech Appian gives her is a composition**, and the series
> cuts it to four lines.

## The naming decision

Every other significant figure in this series has a name, invented or historical. She does not,
because the sources did not trouble to record it, and the series makes that silence visible instead
of papering over it. She is credited as **"the wife of Hasdrubal."** In ch. 200's closing narration
Polybius notes, in one clause, that he never learned her name and did not think to ask at the time.

This rhymes with the unnamed family above the Bagradas in the final image, and with the eighty women
taken off the Cyprus shore in ch. 6 — the three points in the series where it says out loud that the
record is made by people who were not paying attention to everyone in the room.

## Canonical description

A Carthaginian woman of the governing class in her mid-thirties, who has spent three years in a city
under siege running a household that no longer has anything in it, and who has watched her husband
maintain his beard and his purple cloak while other people's children starved. She is not a warrior
and never touches a weapon before the last page. She is composed, dry, and extremely quiet — the
series builds her across two earlier appearances precisely so that ch. 200 is the payoff of a person
rather than the arrival of a symbol.

## Character

- **Want:** for the three years to have meant something.
- **Need:** for her husband to be what he told everyone he was. He is not.
- **Flaw:** none that the series is interested in. She is not the subject of a moral argument.
- **Speech register:** flat, plain, unrhetorical — the exact opposite of her husband's. **Four lines
  in ch. 200 and eleven in the whole series.**
- **Body language:** still. She is the only person in Book Eight who never hurries.

## The rhyme — binding on the art department

Chapter 200's staging **reproduces ch. 18 exactly**: the same hill, the same framing, the same
lens, a woman going into a fire of her own choosing in front of the men who caused it.

- **In ch. 18** it works. Elissa's death is a legal instrument: it binds the chiefs by blood-guilt,
  buys the freehold, and founds a city that lasts six hundred and seventy-nine years.
- **In ch. 200** it binds nobody, buys nothing, saves no one, and is watched by a hundred thousand
  people who will never speak Punic again.

That is the argument of the entire series, delivered without a word of dialogue, by a composition
the audience last saw a hundred and eighty-two chapters earlier.

## Do-not-drift list

1. **Never named.** Not in dialogue, not in credits, not in merchandise.
2. **Build her in ch. 193 and 198 or ch. 200 does not work.** She cannot arrive in the last chapter.
3. **She is not defiant-heroic.** No raised chin, no wind, no dramatic lighting. She is tired and
   she is dressed for it.
4. **The children are on screen and are people.** They have appeared before, briefly, in ch. 198.
   The series does not cut away, and it does not linger.
5. Her death is **not** a comment on her husband's cowardice alone. She says why, and the reason is
   about the city, not about him.

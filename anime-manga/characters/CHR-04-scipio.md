# CHR-04 — PUBLIUS CORNELIUS SCIPIO (AFRICANUS)

## Identity
- **ID:** CHR-04 · **Name:** Publius Cornelius Scipio `[A]`; *Africanus* from 201 BC
- **Role:** second protagonist of Books Six–Seven, from ch. 163
- **First appearance:** ch. 146, aged 17, unnamed in the chapter
- **Status:** `PROPOSED`

## Canonical description

A tall, fair, open-faced Roman aristocrat who is better at being liked than anyone else in the
series and knows exactly what that is worth. Where Hannibal is under-dressed and still, Scipio is
well-turned-out and expressive: he gestures, he addresses crowds, he lets himself be seen praying
alone on the Capitol before dawn because it is good for morale and because he may also mean it — the
series never decides which, and neither did Rome. He is the only man in the story who does what
Hannibal does *and* can manage a senate. He is also the man who burns two sleeping camps during
peace talks, and the series does not let him off.

## Character

- **Want:** to end the war in Africa, personally, and be the man who did it.
- **Need:** to be believed — by his soldiers, by the Iberians, by the Senate. Being believed is his
  actual weapon, and it is the one thing Cato can take from him.
- **Flaw:** he cannot tell the difference between what is true and what he needs to be true.
  The dream of Neptune (ch. 163) and the tide table arrive in the same afternoon.
- **Fear:** Cannae. He was there, at twenty. He never speaks of it and he never fights a battle
  without an escape plan for his own centre.
- **Speech register:** fluent, warm, structured — the only character in the series who is a natural
  public speaker. Uses "we." Names individual soldiers.
- **Body language:** open hands, forward lean, touches people's shoulders. **Everything Hannibal
  doesn't do.**

## Arc

Starts as a frightened seventeen-year-old doing something reckless for his father. Survives the two
worst defeats in Roman history before he is twenty-one. Is given Spain at twenty-five because no one
else will take it, and there invents — by studying his enemy in complete detail — a Roman way of
making war that is flexible, fast, political, and explicitly modelled on Hannibal's. Wins the war.
Is then prosecuted by his own city for twenty years over money, tears up his accounts rather than
justify himself, and dies on a farm in the same season as the man he beat.

## Do-not-drift list

1. **He must be likeable.** If the audience roots against him at Zama, Book Seven has failed.
2. **He must not be clean.** The camp-burning (ch. 170) is filmed in full.
3. **Visual opposition to Hannibal is systematic:** tall/short, fair/dark, red cloak/grey cloak,
   speaks to crowds/speaks to individuals, uncovered head/borrowed Roman helmet. Hold it in every
   shared panel.
4. He is **younger than the audience expects** in every chapter before 172.

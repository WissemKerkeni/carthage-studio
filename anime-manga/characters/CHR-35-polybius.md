# CHR-35 — POLYBIUS OF MEGALOPOLIS

## Identity
- **ID:** CHR-35 · **Name:** Polybius of Megalopolis `[A]` — a **real historical person**, and the
  single most important source for everything in this series
- **Role:** **narrator of Book Eight** (ch. 185–200); on screen from ch. 196
- **First appearance:** ch. 185 (voice), ch. 196 (in person)
- **Status:** `PROPOSED`

> **Evidence note.** Polybius is real `[A]`: an Achaean statesman deported to Italy in 167 BC as one
> of a thousand hostages, held seventeen years, who became the friend and teacher of Scipio
> Aemilianus, was present at the destruction of Carthage, and wrote forty books of which five survive
> complete. He records the Homer scene in ch. 200 himself. His birth year (c. 200 BC) and death
> (c. 118 BC, reportedly from a fall from a horse at 82) are `[C]`. His interior life here is `[F]`.

## Canonical description

A compact, upright Greek gentleman in his fifties: a former cavalry commander of the Achaean League
who has spent seventeen years as a well-treated prisoner in the houses of the people who conquered
his country, and has made a career out of being useful to them. He is the opposite of Sosylos in
every way that matters — where Sosylos was a hired tutor who loved his subject and vanished,
Polybius is a political professional who is clear-eyed about his own position and says so in his
first lines on screen. He rides, he hunts, he inspects ground personally, he corrects other people's
distances. He is not a sympathetic narrator. He is an honest one, which is rarer and colder.

## Character

- **Want:** to explain to Greeks how Rome conquered the world in fifty-three years, so that they stop
  being surprised by it.
- **Need:** to be honest about the fact that he is writing in the conqueror's house, on the
  conqueror's food. He manages it, which is the most admirable thing about him.
- **Flaw:** he believes that understanding a catastrophe is a form of doing something about it.
- **Fear:** that he is exactly what his countrymen say he is.
- **Speech register:** precise, technical, faintly lecturing; gives distances and numbers; flags his
  own sourcing (*I was there; I had this from; I do not believe the figure*). **Contrast with
  Sosylos:** Sosylos apologises for his uncertainty, Polybius audits it.
- **Body language:** watches ground, not faces. Writes standing up.

## Narrative function

1. **The handover.** Ch. 185's first lines are the series' thesis stated plainly: *Mine will survive.
   I want that understood at the start… because I was in the right house.* The audience has spent
   184 chapters with a narrator whose books were lost; this one survives, and is candid about why.
2. **Carries the bridge from Hannibal to the fall.** He read the bronze tablets at Croton (ch. 185);
   he is the reason anything true is known about the protagonist the audience has just buried.
3. **He is the witness to the last scene.** The Homer exchange in ch. 200 is reported by Polybius
   himself in the real source, to a conversation at which he was the only other person present.
   The series believes him and says so.

## Arc

Deported at about thirty-three with a thousand other men of his class, on a list of names supplied by
Greek collaborators. Makes himself indispensable to the first family in Rome. Spends seventeen years
being unable to go home and becoming, in the process, the only person alive capable of explaining the
machine that took him. Stands beside his pupil at the end of the war he has spent his life studying,
watches a civilisation burn, is asked nothing and volunteers nothing — and then asks the one question
that gets the answer the whole series has been moving toward.

Writes forty books. They survive. That is the tragedy and the point.

## Do-not-drift list

1. **He is not a bystander with a conscience.** He agrees with a great deal of what Rome is doing and
   says so. His honesty is about *sourcing*, not about morality.
2. **Visually the anti-Sosylos:** upright vs stooped, riding vs squinting, standing to write vs bent
   over a desk, clean-shaven vs unkempt. Deliberate.
3. **No armour, no weapon, ever** — including in ch. 198–199, in the middle of the assault.
4. Ch. 200: he asks his question **as a teacher**, not as a mourner. The grief in the frame is
   Scipio's. Polybius is taking notes, and the series lets that be as disturbing as it is.

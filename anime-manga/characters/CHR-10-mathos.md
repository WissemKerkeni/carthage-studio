> crowd. Everything about his village, his family and his interior life is `[F]`.

## Canonical description

A lean, unhurried Libyan infantryman in his late thirties, sun-black, with a soldier's economy of
movement and a farmer's hands. He speaks good Punic, learned over eleven years of service, and that
is exactly the problem: he understands precisely what Carthage says about people like him, in
Carthage's own language, and has stopped finding it bearable. He is not a liberator and the series
never lets him be one — he assents to the torture of seven hundred prisoners to make peace
impossible, and he knows what he is doing when he does it.

## Character

- **Want:** the tribute ended and the levy ended, in writing, with something that holds.
- **Need:** to be answered. Gisco cannot answer him, and that is the moment the war becomes
  inevitable.
- **Flaw:** he understands that the only way to keep seventy thousand frightened men together is to
  make surrender impossible — and he does it, and it also makes victory impossible.
- **Fear:** that after all of it the arrangement will simply resume, one generation later, with
  different names. It does.
- **Speech register:** quiet, level, specific. Talks about harvests, quantities and years. **Never
  makes a rhetorical speech**; his one great scene (ch. 99) is a man in a tent listing facts.
- **Body language:** stands very still while others shout. Squats to talk to people rather than
  standing over them.

## Arc

A man who served Carthage for eleven years and came home to find his village assessed at twice the
tribute, asks — correctly, quietly, through channels — for redress, and is answered with a promise.
He then leads the only rising that ever came close to destroying Carthage from inside Africa, and to
hold it together he authorises an atrocity that guarantees his own death. He is torn apart in a
street by shopkeepers and mothers who are, by that point, entirely justified.

## Do-not-drift list

1. **Not noble, not savage.** He is a professional soldier with a political case.
2. **The Punic is fluent.** His literacy in the coloniser's language is the character.
3. Ch. 112 stays on the **crowd**, not on him. The horror is theirs, not his.

# CHR-02 — HAMILCAR BARCA

## Identity
- **ID:** CHR-02 · **Name:** Hamilcar Barca `[A]` (*Ḥamilqart*, "servant of Melqart"; *Baraq*, lightning)
- **Role:** protagonist of Book Three (part) and Book Four; the father
- **First appearance:** ch. 87 · **Dies:** ch. 127–128 (228 BC)
- **Status:** `PROPOSED`

## Canonical description

A broad, grey-eyed, unhurried man of about thirty at first appearance, with a countryman's build
and a bureaucrat's habits. The most striking thing about him is that he is not striking: he looks
like a competent estate manager, speaks quietly, and listens to sergeants. He wins for six years on
Mount Eryx with an army nobody is paying, loses nothing, and is ordered by his own government to
sign a surrender — and the face he makes when the order arrives is the one the series returns to
every time the Hundred and Four is mentioned. He is a genuinely great commander, a genuinely
ruthless colonial administrator, and a father who resolved a problem of political succession by
putting his nine-year-old son's hand on a sacrificial victim.

## Character

- **Want:** a base of money and manpower outside Carthage's political reach, from which Rome's
  settlement can eventually be reversed.
- **Need:** to be trusted by his own city. He never is, and after 241 BC he stops asking.
- **Flaw:** he solves political problems with institutions he controls personally — which is exactly
  what the Hundred and Four was created to prevent, and he is not wrong that they will destroy him
  if he does otherwise.
- **Fear:** dying on a cross in the marketplace like Bomilcar. Stated once, ch. 114, flatly.
- **Speech register:** short, mild, administrative. Gives orders as though asking for a favour.
  Never threatens; the threat is understood. His cruelty is delivered in the same tone as his
  logistics (ch. 107, the elephants).
- **Body language:** economical. Sits when others stand. Reads while people argue.

## Arc

Starts as the best officer of a losing war who believes the war is still winnable if the government
will fund it. Learns on Eryx that his enemy is not the Roman army but Rome's capacity to absorb
defeat. Is ordered to surrender undefeated, resigns rather than disarm his men, and thereby causes
— directly — the Truceless War, in which he saves the city by methods that make him unemployable
anywhere except at the head of a private army. Takes that army to Iberia, builds a state, and dies
in a river at about forty-seven, leaving three sons, a son-in-law, a silver mine and a war.

## Do-not-drift list

1. **Never heroic-posed.** No windswept promontory shots. He is a manager.
2. **The beard stays.** He is the last Barcid who is a *Carthaginian gentleman* by appearance.
3. **He is not angry.** Artists reach for fury when they hear "Barca." The correct expression is
   patience, and it is worse.
4. Death panel: **both traditions in one frame** — drowning, with a burning ox-cart in the shallows
   behind him. Do not resolve it. `[C]`

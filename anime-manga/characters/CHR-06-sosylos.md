## Canonical description

A dry, short-sighted, professionally sceptical Greek of about forty when he takes the post, who came
west because the pay was good and stayed for thirty-odd years because the material was. Ink on the
fingers; squints; carries tablets everywhere and is visibly restraining himself from correcting
people's grammar. He is not a wise mentor. He is a working historian embedded with a subject, aware
that this compromises him, and saying so in the narration — which is what makes him the right
narrator for a story whose sources are all compromised.

## Character

- **Want:** to write down what actually happened, in a world where nobody else is going to.
- **Need:** to be read. He will not be.
- **Flaw:** he loves his subject and knows it disqualifies him. Says so twice.
- **Speech register (in narration):** first person, past tense, conversational, self-correcting.
  Habitually flags his own uncertainty: *I was not there. I had this from a man who. Treat it as you
  please.* **This is the series' epistemic engine** and must never be softened into omniscience.
- **Speech register (in scene):** pedantic, mildly sarcastic, and unexpectedly brave twice.

## Narrative function

1. **Licenses the series' honesty.** He can say "the sources disagree" out loud without breaking
   the drama, because he *is* a source.
2. **Covers the 600-year span.** He narrates Books One to Four as reconstruction — openly, as a
   historian working from documents and hearsay — and Books Five to Seven as testimony.
3. **Hands over.** Ch. 184 is his, alone: seven finished scrolls and a cord with nineteen knots.
   **It is not the end of the series.** In ch. 185 his books are sold for the blank backs of the
   sheets and **Polybius** — who survives because he lived in the winners' house, and says so —
   narrates the last sixteen chapters. That handover is the series' closing argument.

## Arc

Takes a tutoring job for money. Realises within two years that he is standing next to the largest
historical subject of his lifetime. Spends thirty years taking notes in the corner of the most
famous campaign in antiquity. Finishes seven books in exile, alone, knowing they will not survive,
and finishes them anyway — which is the only unambiguously admirable act in a series that has
carefully avoided providing any others.

## Do-not-drift list

1. **Not a wise old mentor.** He never advises Hannibal on anything but Greek prose.
2. **He is present at battles and does nothing.** No last-minute heroism, ever.
3. **The narration is always dated to the writing, not the action** — he is an old man remembering,
   and occasionally gets things wrong on screen, and the series lets the wrongness stand.
4. In ch. 1 he is a silhouette at a desk. **Do not reveal the face until ch. 124.**

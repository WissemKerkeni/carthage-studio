# CHR-03 — ELISSA

## Identity
- **ID:** CHR-03 · **Name:** Elissa of Tyre `[C]` · **Also called:** Dido (Greek — used only by
  non-Carthaginians and by later generations retelling the legend); "the queen," which she never is
- **Role:** protagonist of Book One
- **First appearance:** ch. 2 · **Dies:** ch. 18 (c. 820 BC)
- **Status:** `PROPOSED`

> **Evidence note.** Her historicity is disputed `[C]`. Pygmalion is attested; the king-list
> preserved by Josephus includes her; the ox-hide and the Aeneas romance are certainly invention.
> This series treats her as a real political actor and shows the legend being composed around her
> in ch. 21–22, twenty years after her death.

## Canonical description

A tall, thin woman of about thirty with a priest's training and no army, who does the arithmetic
faster than the men around her and has learned to say the result last so that someone else can
propose it. Levantine dark; hair covered in public and pinned up in private; expensive clothes that
get steadily worse over eighteen chapters until she is dressed like everybody else. She smiles in
negotiations and nowhere else. She is not maternal, not romantic, and not a martyr — and the city
she founds will remember her as all three within one generation, which is the point of Book One.

## Character

- **Want:** to keep a hundred and forty people alive and out of her brother's reach.
- **Need:** to build something that will not be inherited, sold, split or married away.
- **Flaw:** she manufactures loyalty instead of earning it (the sacks, ch. 4), and it works, and
  it sets the pattern the city follows for six hundred years.
- **Fear:** that the settlement is a possession — and that possessions get divided among heirs.
- **Speech register:** temple-trained. Formal, rhythmic, legal. Quotes contracts. Says *we are
  owed* and *we owe* more than any other phrase, and both halves of the series turn on those words.
- **Body language:** perfectly still in public; paces alone.

## Arc

Starts as the second-most powerful person in the richest city in the world, and loses it in a night.
Turns a flight into a colony by making return impossible. Negotiates a tenancy and never lets anyone
call it anything else. And when a marriage is proposed that would end the rent and also end the
city's independence, she turns her own death into a binding obligation on the men who demanded her —
which works, and which is remembered, within twenty years, as a love story.

## Do-not-drift list

1. **Not a warrior queen.** No sword, no armour, no battlefield. Her weapons are contracts.
2. **The purple fades.** Track the robe's colour across Book One; it is the Book's only visual arc.
3. **She is not beautiful in the panel's opinion.** No glamour lighting, no wind machine.
4. **The ox-hide never happens on screen.** It appears only in ch. 21, told by a priest to children,
   while the woman who was actually there stands at the back and says nothing.

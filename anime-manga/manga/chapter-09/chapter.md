## Role in the story

The arc's aftermath chapter, and a hard rule protects it: **no aftermath chapter may ever be cut**
(`story-bible.md` §9.5). Four chapters of consequential decisions — the sacks, the office, the
eighty, the rent — and this is the one that says what they cost people who did not take them.

It also closes the trap sprung in ch. 4. When the knife fight about going home is finally settled,
it is settled by arithmetic, not by loyalty: **they have all been seen throwing the treasury into
the sea.** Elissa's method from chapter 4 holds, five chapters later, in the dark, without her
having to be present.

## Summary

The first winter nearly finishes them. A bad well kills three. There is a fight, with knives, about
whether to sail back to Tyre and throw themselves on Pygmalion's mercy — and the fight is settled by
the simple observation that there is nowhere to go back to, because of what they were all seen to do.

They dig a harbour, badly. They plant, worse.

## Opens on

The well. A wide, flat, unremarkable panel of people drawing water — the thing that is going to kill
three of them, shot without emphasis.

## Ends on

Mud, and people still there.

## Reader takeaway

1. Founding a city is mostly mud, bad water, and people dying of ordinary things.
2. Nobody can go home, and the reason is what Elissa did in chapter 4.
3. They stay anyway — not from hope, but from arithmetic.

## Information withheld

- Any reassurance. **The chapter does not promise that it works out.** The reader knows Carthage exists; the people on screen do not, and the chapter is staged from their position.

## The knife fight

The arc's only violence, and it is small, ugly, indoors, and over in three panels.

It must not be exciting. `style-guide.md` §12.8 — no panel that makes killing look weightless —
applies doubly here, and the fight should be hard to follow in the way real fights are.

**The settling line is the chapter's spine**, and it is not a speech. Somebody points out that they
were all seen throwing the treasury into the sea. That is it. The fight stops, not because anyone
is persuaded, but because the alternative has been arithmetic-ed away.

**Who says it is an open question** (below). It should probably not be Elissa.

## Craft notes

- **Rain and mud are the chapter's texture** — the one place in Book One where `style-guide.md`'s dust-as-weather is replaced by water. Heavy blacks, 60% tone everywhere, almost no paper white.
- **No deathbed scenes.** The three deaths are shown as illness and then as burial. Nobody gets last words.
- **Elissa is visibly not in charge of this.** She can negotiate a tenancy and cannot make a well clean. The chapter is the first time the audience sees the limit of what she is good at, and it sets up ch. 16–17.
- Ch. 10 follows immediately and is quiet and small. **These two chapters are a pair** and should be storyboarded together.

## Open questions

1. **Are the three dead named?** Proposal: **two named, one not**, and the unnamed one is the one the chapter lingers on. Naming all three sentimentalises it; naming none makes them scenery.
2. **Who settles the knife fight?** Proposal: **Yatonbaal.** He is a rope-maker who states conditions rather than opinions (`CHR-34` speech register), he was on the deck in ch. 4, and it gives him the weight he needs before ch. 10 hands him the arc's final image. **Not Elissa** — the point is that her method works without her.

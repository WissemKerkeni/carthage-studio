# Chapter 05 — CYPRUS

**Status:** `PROPOSED`. Source: `THE-STORY.md` §5–6 · `arcs.md` Arc 1.
Book One · Arc 1 · 18 pages. The shortest chapter in the arc.

## Role in the story

**The first Carthaginian office is sold before Carthage exists**, and the transaction takes less
than a minute.

A priest of Astarte comes aboard and names his price: the priesthood of his god in the new city, for
his line, for ever. Elissa agrees immediately. The series will spend six hundred chapters on the
consequences of a state whose offices are purchased (`world.md` — Aristotle's complaint as running
joke and running horror), and it starts here, casually, between two competent people who both
think they are getting a bargain.

The chapter is deliberately light and quick — a breather between the sacks and the eighty women —
and it is the last time anything in Book One is easy.

## Summary

Cyprus. The ships put in for water and repair. A priest of Astarte comes aboard, assesses the
situation in about four seconds, and makes an offer: his god's cult in whatever they build, his
family's in perpetuity, and in exchange his blessing, his local standing, and his silence about
eight Tyrian hulls with no cargo manifest.

Elissa agrees in under a minute. Neither of them writes anything down.

## Opens on

Water casks going aboard. Ordinary, procedural, almost cheerful after chapter 4.

## Ends on

A handshake, or its Phoenician equivalent — and then a held panel of the two of them not looking at
each other, both privately satisfied. **The last panel is a joke the audience will not get for two
hundred chapters.**

## Reader takeaway

1. Offices in this city are property, and they always have been.
2. Elissa buys loyalty the way she manufactures it — quickly, and by giving away something that does not exist yet.
3. The new settlement now has a god it did not choose.

## Information withheld

- That the same deal structure destroys Carthaginian politics for six centuries. **Never signalled.** No narration box explains the significance. The audience finds out by living with it.

## The negotiation

Eighteen pages, and the deal itself takes four. The chapter's shape is deliberately lopsided:

| Pages | Content |
|---|---|
| 1–6 | Cyprus. Water, repair, the crews recovering. The first daylight since ch. 3 |
| 7–10 | The priest arrives and watches. He is doing what Zakarbaal did in ch. 1 — **assessing people at work before speaking** — and the visual echo should be deliberate |
| 11–14 | **The offer, the acceptance.** Four pages. Almost no reaction from anyone |
| 15–18 | Aftermath. The crews' indifference. Nobody thinks anything has happened |

**The echo of ch. 1 is the chapter's best idea.** A powerful man comes to working people and makes
a quiet proposal that changes everything. Frame the priest's arrival with the same staging as
Zakarbaal's — approach down a line, a stop, a period of watching — so the reader feels the rhyme
without being told.

## The purple

Colour-arc step 3 of 5, holding. Salt-damaged, drying in daylight for the first time. The reader
should register that it is becoming ordinary clothing.

## Craft notes

- **Daylight and white.** After ch. 4's black gutters, this chapter is blown-out Mediterranean noon. The tonal whiplash is the point of putting it here.
- **The priest is not sinister.** No shadowed framing, no knowing smile. He is a professional making a sound business proposal, and he is right to.
- **Nothing is written down.** Hold a panel on the absence of a document. It will matter in Arc 2, when the council tries to work out what Elissa actually promised.

## Open question

in two or three unremarked background panels here — carrying a basket, doing someone's washing —
would make ch. 6 substantially worse to watch, in the way the series generally prefers.

**Proposal: yes.** Three background panels, no emphasis, no eyeline to camera. Confirm, because it
changes how ch. 5's beach is staged and blocked.

# Chapter 08 — RENT

**Status:** `PROPOSED`. Source: `THE-STORY.md` §7–9 · `arcs.md` Arc 1, marked ★.
Book One · Arc 1 · 30 pages. **The most important chapter in Book One.**

> `THE-STORY.md`: *"Carthage paid rent to the Libyans for its land, reportedly until the mid-sixth
> century BC. This is the single most important fact in Book One and the series builds the whole of
> Book Two on the moment it stops being paid."* `[A]`

## Role in the story

Everything the series is about is decided in this chapter, in a tent, by people haggling.

**No ox-hide.** The famous story — Elissa buying the hill with a strip cut from a single hide — is a
Greek pun on *byrsa*, and the series refuses it here and then shows it being invented in ch. 21 by
people who were present. What actually happens is eleven days of hard bargaining over grazing
rights, water, lagoon fish, timber, war-shelter obligations, and above all **the annual payment**.

**Not a purchase. A tenancy.** If the audience takes one sentence out of Book One, it is that one.

## Summary

Eleven days, conducted through two interpreters and a Libyan trader who is cheating everyone.
Elissa does what her husband's temple had done for four hundred years: she buys — or rather, she
rents, because the chiefs will not sell and do not conceive of selling.

Tikat is there. She is twenty, the daughter of one of the chiefs, and she is present because her
father wanted someone who could count to be watching the foreigners count.

What she remembered afterwards, and told her grandchildren, was not the bargaining. It was that at
the end of it the foreign queen asked — through two interpreters, badly — what the hill was called.

**Nobody had ever asked. It was the hill. It was called the hill.**

The foreigners named it Bosra, meaning citadel. In six hundred years a Greek will hear that word,
think it is his own word for an ox-hide, and invent a story good enough to outlive every person in
this chapter.

## Opens on

A tally. Two sets of counting — Punic reckoning and Libyan reckoning, side by side, not matching.
**Chapter 2 opened on a column of figures and so does this one**, deliberately: the series' two
halves, the flight and the founding, both begin with arithmetic.

## Ends on

The name. *"What is the hill called?"* — and the answer, and the small silence after it.

## Reader takeaway

1. Carthage rents its ground. It does not own it.
2. The Libyans are not naive and are not cheated — they drive a hard bargain and get an annual income. **They are also selling something they do not believe is a thing that can be sold**, and both facts are true at once.
3. The hill had no name, and the name the foreigners gave it will one day become a lie that outlives everyone.

## Information withheld

- That Carthage will stop paying. **Never hinted.** The payoff is ch. 28, two hundred pages later, and it must land as a betrayal the reader did not see coming.
- The ox-hide. **It does not exist yet.** Nobody in this chapter has heard it.

## The craft problem: thirty pages of haggling

This is the chapter that decides whether the series can do what it claims to do — make
administration dramatic. The tools:

**1. Six negotiating positions, tracked visually.** Grazing, water, fish, timber, war-shelter,
payment. Proposal: a recurring **tally object** — stones, or marks on a board — so the reader can
see at a glance which points are settled and which are open, on every day of the eleven.

**2. The eleven days are compressed to six staged rounds**, each with its own weather, light and
exhaustion level. Day one and day eleven are drawn from the same angle, and everyone looks worse.

**3. Mistranslation is the content, not the colour.** `story-bible.md` §9.9. At least two points
turn on a word going through three languages and arriving wrong. **The reader is shown the error
and the participants are not** — which means the reader is ahead of everyone in the tent, and that
is what makes thirty pages of talking tense.

**4. The trader is cheating everyone and the audience can see it.** He is not a villain; he is
taking a margin from both sides, which is his job. He is also the only person in the tent who
understands all three languages, which makes him the most powerful person present and the only one
nobody is watching.

**5. Nobody raises their voice.** Not once, in thirty pages.

## Tikat's POV — why the chapter is hers

Elissa is the one negotiating, and giving her the POV would make the chapter a story about a clever
woman getting a good deal. From Tikat's side it is a story about a family signing away something
they do not understand as ownable, to people who understand nothing else.

`story-bible.md`'s theme **Empire from below** — *every Carthaginian triumph is somebody's harvest* —
is carried by the tent of Iyarut across the whole series. **This is that line's origin chapter.**

Tikat is twenty, competent, numerate, and the only person on her side of the tent tracking the
foreigners' arithmetic. She is not fooled. She is outmatched on a point of concept, not of
intelligence: her father is selling access to land, and Elissa is buying a permanent claim on it,
and both of them think they have agreed the same thing.

## The name — the chapter's last page

Four things happen in one panel, and it is the best beat in the arc:

- A powerful person asks a question nobody has thought to ask.
- The answer is that there is no answer — *it was the hill*.
- A name is imposed, casually, by people who need places to have names.
- The audience is told, in narration, that this word will become a lie that outlives everyone in the tent.

**This is the only place in Arc 1 where narration is permitted to reach forward in time.** It is
earned because the entire chapter has been about the gap between what happened and what will be
said about it — and because `story-bible.md` §12 ends the whole series on exactly that problem.

## The purple

Colour-arc step 5 of 5. **Gone.** Undyed wool, a single gold pin. Elissa negotiates the most
important agreement in Carthaginian history dressed like everybody else — and that is a choice she
made, not a circumstance she suffered.

## Page budget

**30 pages.** Roughly: 3 (the two tallies) + 4 (arrival, the tent, who is present) + 16 (six
negotiating rounds) + 4 (agreement, and what each side thinks it agreed) + 3 (the name).

## Craft notes

- **Interiors and one exterior.** The tent is the set; the hill is seen from it, through the opening, in almost every scene, and it is never entered.
- **Hands again** — the Book One vocabulary. Tikat's hands should be established here as distinct from all four established pairs: herding and counting, not rope, not laundry, not priesthood.
- **Do not cut this chapter short in a page-budget squeeze.** If the arc has to lose pages, take them from ch. 2, 5, 7 and 9. Chapter 8 is the one that cannot shrink.

# Chapter 1 — THE ROPE WALK

**Status:** `PROPOSED`. Source: `story/THE-STORY.md` §1 and `story/arcs.md` Arc 1.
Book One · Arc 1 · marked ★ (must exist in any cut of the series).

---

## Role in the story

This is the first chapter of a 200-chapter series whose protagonist is not seen for another
114 chapters. It has one job, and it is not "hook the reader with action".

**It must make the audience trust that the series knows what it is doing.** It does that by being
completely specific about a trade, a smell, and a family — and by ending on a question that has
obviously dangerous implications which are never explained.

The source's own production note is binding: *no violence, no exposition of politics, and no named
historical event.* Three things get established:

1. **The smell of the city** — Tyre as a real place, rich and filthy and frightened.
2. **The trade of the family the audience will follow for six hundred years** — rope, in working detail.
3. **The fact that something is being prepared in secret.**

It also plants the series' frame: Sosylos, an old man at an empty desk, telling the audience in the
first thirty seconds that everything they are about to watch was lost.

## Summary

Tyre, c. 825 BC. An old Greek at a desk says he wrote seven books and none survived, and that he
will begin where his subject would have begun — with the ground. The ground is a city that stinks
of dead shellfish, pays tribute to Assyria, and contains a four-hundred-pace lane where the family
of Yatonbaal lays cordage, walking backwards, as it has for six generations. Elishat, fourteen,
splices better than her father because her hands are small. The second man in the city — Zakarbaal,
priest of Melqart — comes down the lane alone, without a retinue, lifting the hem of a very good
robe over the tar. He watches them work. Then he asks how much rope they can make in a month, and
whether they can do it without anyone knowing.

Elishat's father does not look up. *"That depends on what you tell the harbour master."*

*"Nothing,"* says the priest. *"I intend to tell him nothing at all."*

The chapter ends on the rope.

## Opens on

An empty desk in a bare room, six hundred years later, and a voice. **Not** on Tyre.
The reader does not know who is speaking, and will not learn until chapter 124.

## Ends on

The rope itself — yarn laying into strand, strand into cable — and no resolution.
The final page is wordless. Nothing has happened, and the reader knows something has.

## Reader takeaway

By the last page the reader knows three things, and nothing more:

1. Rope is made by walking backwards, and this family is very good at it.
2. Tyre is rich, filthy, crowded, and pays tribute to someone frightening.
3. A powerful man wants a large quantity of rope, and wants no one to know.

## Information withheld

- Who the narrator is. (Revealed ch. 124.)
- That the rope is for an escape. (Ch. 2.)
- That Zakarbaal will be murdered. (Ch. 3.)
- That Elissa exists. (Ch. 2.)
- That any of this leads to a city, a war, or Hannibal. **Chapter 1 never gestures at the future.**
  No "little did they know". No foreshadowing beyond the narrator's opening admission of loss.

## The chapter's visual argument

Two pairs of hands.

Elishat's are small, tar-black in the nail beds, callused across the finger bases, and expert.
Zakarbaal's are soft, unmarked except for a stylus indentation and a censer burn, and they lift a
hem clear of the tar. The chapter is built so that the reader compares them without being told to —
Elishat watches hands rather than faces (`CHR-12` do-not-drift §4), so the camera follows her
eyeline and arrives at his.

**This is the panel-level thesis of the whole series: the people who make the rope, and the people
who buy it.** The art department should treat the hands panels as the chapter's most important
images, above any establishing shot.

## The clock

There is no plot in this chapter, so time is carried visually: the temple's shadow crosses the rope
walk on a diagonal and moves through the chapter (`LOC-01`). It is established on page 3 and has
moved measurably by the last page. **This is continuity and must be tracked panel by panel** — see
the shadow column in `page-plan.md`.

## Page budget

**42 pages.** `PROPOSED`.

Rationale: a first chapter needs room to be unhurried, because being unhurried *is* the promise the
series is making. Breakdown in `page-plan.md`: 4 (frame) + 8 (Tyre) + 12 (the work) + 14 (the
priest) + 4 (the rope).

A 32-page cut exists: compress the Tyre establishing sequence from 8 pages to 4, and the work
sequence from 12 to 8. It is worse, and it is available if the serialisation demands it.

> **Against the restructure's budget.** `arcs.md` now sets **Books One–Two at an average of 24
> pages** per chapter. 42 is well above that. This is deliberate and, I think, correct: a series
> opener is the one chapter that buys the audience's patience for the following 199, and across
> Book One's 22 chapters a single 42-page opener moves the average by less than one page. **But it
> is an exception to a budget the author has just set, so it is flagged rather than assumed.**
> If the 24-page average is hard, take the 32-page cut; below 32 the chapter stops working, because
> pp. 30–33 (the silence) and the pp. 13–21 process sequence are the two things it is made of.
